If you've spent weeks learning something online and now need to prove it to an employer, a client, or even yourself, the honest answer is that a certificate is rarely the strongest evidence you have. What actually convinces people is seeing you do the thing — a project, a piece of writing, working code, a real result. [Teyro](/onboarding/0) is structured around this idea: the Apply phase of every lesson has you produce something real, not just finish a module.

## Direct Answer: Work Beats Certificates, Usually

The strongest proof you learned a skill online is a portfolio of real, applied work — projects, writing, code, case studies — because it demonstrates capability directly rather than attendance. Certificates can support that proof as a secondary credential, but for most fields outside specific technical or compliance certifications, actual demonstrated output carries more weight with employers and clients.

| Type of proof | What it demonstrates | Employer/client weight |
|---|---|---|
| Certificate of completion | You finished a course | Low-moderate, field-dependent |
| Specific technical certification | Verified, standardized competence | High in certain fields (IT, finance) |
| Portfolio of real projects | Actual applied capability | High across most fields |
| Public work (writing, GitHub, case studies) | Capability plus initiative | High, and self-verifying |

## Why Certificates Alone Don't Prove Much

A certificate mostly confirms one thing: you were present for and completed a course. It says very little about whether you can actually do the underlying skill under real conditions, and most employers and clients know this, which is part of why certificate-only resumes often get less traction than people expect. This isn't a knock on the courses themselves — plenty are genuinely good — it's a limitation of what a completion certificate can honestly signal on its own.

This gap is worth internalizing early, because it changes how you should spend your learning time. If the end goal is "prove I can do this," time spent purely accumulating certificates is less valuable than time spent producing something real with the skill, even a small, imperfect example.

## Building Proof as You Learn, Not After

The strongest approach is to generate proof continuously, as a byproduct of learning, rather than trying to build a portfolio retroactively once you feel "ready." This is exactly why a Learn → Apply structure matters more than it might seem: every time you apply a new concept to a real exercise, you're creating a small artifact — a snippet of code, a draft pitch, a mini case study — that can eventually become part of your proof, without needing a separate portfolio-building project later.

Concretely, this could look like:

- Saving every coding exercise you complete, even small ones, in a public repository.
- Turning a writing skill's practice exercises into a small public blog or sample set.
- Documenting a business skill exercise (a pitch, a plan) as a case study you can show later.
- Keeping a simple log of what you built or solved each week, even informally.

None of this requires waiting until you're "done" learning — the proof accumulates alongside the learning itself, which is both more efficient and more honest than trying to retroactively demonstrate skills you learned passively.

## When a Certificate Actually Does Matter

It's worth being fair here: in certain fields, a specific certification genuinely does carry weight — security certifications, some finance and PM credentials, and other standardized, third-party-verified qualifications are treated differently than a generic course completion badge, because they're independently recognized and often tied to a real exam. If you're in one of those fields, pursuing the recognized credential alongside your applied work makes sense. For most general skill-building — communication, coding fundamentals, business skills — the applied work will do more of the convincing.
