Most people who "can't cook" have actually just never learned technique — they've only ever followed recipes, which is a completely different skill from understanding why a recipe works. The moment you're out of the recipe's exact ingredients or need to improvise, that gap shows immediately. [Teyro](/onboarding/0) approaches cooking from the technique side, because that's the part that actually makes someone a capable cook rather than a recipe-follower.

## Direct Answer: Recipes vs. Technique

Recipe apps are great once you can already cook — they give you specific dishes to follow. But if you want to become someone who can cook confidently without a recipe, you need technique: knife skills, heat control, seasoning logic, and a handful of foundational methods. Gamified technique-focused apps teach that layer; recipe apps assume you already have it.

| App Type | Teaches | Best For |
|---|---|---|
| Gamified technique app (Teyro) | Knife skills, heat control, seasoning, foundational methods | Becoming a self-sufficient cook |
| Recipe apps | Specific dishes, step-by-step instructions | Cooking a particular meal well |
| Cooking YouTube channels | Visual technique demos, personality-driven | Free, but unstructured |
| In-person cooking classes | Real-time feedback on technique | Correcting physical habits (knife grip, pan control) |

## Why Following Recipes Doesn't Teach You to Cook

There's a specific ceiling that recipe-followers hit: they can make the ten dishes they have recipes for, and they're lost the moment they need to substitute an ingredient, adjust for a different quantity, or improvise with what's in the fridge. That ceiling exists because recipes tell you *what* to do without explaining *why* — why you sear meat before braising it, why you salt pasta water generously, why acid balances a rich sauce.

Technique-focused learning fixes this by teaching the underlying logic rather than a fixed sequence of steps. Once you understand that browning creates flavor through the Maillard reaction, that salt draws out and concentrates flavor, and that acid cuts through richness, you can look at almost any ingredients and improvise a reasonable dish — which is the actual definition of "knowing how to cook."

This is well-suited to short, structured lessons: a concept like "why you rest meat after cooking" is a self-contained idea you can learn, understand the reasoning for, and immediately apply the next time you cook. Teyro's Learn → Apply → Reflect → Deepen structure fits this shape — Learn covers the technique or principle, Apply has you plan how you'd use it in an actual dish, Reflect connects it to cooking you've already done, and Deepen goes into the food science for people who are curious why it works.

## Core Cooking Skills Worth Learning First

1. **Knife skills** — basic cuts (dice, julienne, chop) that make prep faster and more even, which affects cooking results.
2. **Heat control** — understanding what low, medium, and high heat actually do to different ingredients.
3. **Seasoning logic** — the interplay of salt, acid, fat, and heat that most good cooking comes down to.
4. **Foundational methods** — sautéing, roasting, braising, and a basic sauce or two (a pan sauce, a vinaigrette).
5. **Ingredient substitution logic** — understanding what a given ingredient is doing in a dish so you can swap it intelligently.

## Gamified Technique Learning vs. Cooking Classes

| Factor | Gamified App | In-Person Cooking Class |
|---|---|---|
| Cost | Usually low / freemium | Often $50-150+ per class |
| Access | On-demand, any time | Scheduled, limited availability |
| Feedback | Conceptual, self-assessed | Real-time correction from an instructor |
| Best for | Building the mental model behind cooking | Correcting hands-on technique (knife grip, pan handling) |

Neither replaces the other completely. A gamified app is the more realistic starting point for most people because it's free or low-cost and fits into small pockets of time, while a class is worth it later for hands-on correction of physical habits that are hard to self-diagnose.

## A Realistic Path to Becoming a Better Cook

- **Week 1-2**: Learn knife skills and heat control basics in short daily sessions, and actually practice them while cooking whatever you'd normally make.
- **Week 3**: Add seasoning logic — start tasting and adjusting dishes as you cook rather than only following a recipe's exact measurements.
- **Week 4+**: Try cooking one meal a week without a recipe, using only technique and what's in your fridge. This is the real test of whether the skill has transferred.
