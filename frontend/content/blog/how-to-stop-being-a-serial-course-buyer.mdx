There's a specific, sheepish feeling that comes with realizing your course platform account has eleven purchases and two completions, and it's more common than the marketing for any of those courses would ever admit. Buying a course feels like progress — you've taken action, made a commitment, told yourself a new story about who you're becoming — and that feeling is real even when zero actual learning has happened yet. This post is about breaking that specific pattern, not about willpower in the abstract, and it connects to why a completion-first format like [Teyro](/onboarding/0) approaches the problem differently than another course purchase would.

## Direct Answer: How to Stop Being a Serial Course-Buyer

Stop being a serial course-buyer by pausing new purchases until you finish (or deliberately abandon) what you already own, picking one thing to focus on at a time instead of several, and switching to a format built around short, consistent completion rather than large, front-loaded content libraries that reward buying over finishing. The purchase itself isn't the problem — treating purchase as equivalent to progress is.

| Old Pattern | Replacement Habit |
| :--- | :--- |
| Buy a new course when motivated | Add it to a waitlist; don't buy until you finish your current one |
| Own 5-10 unfinished courses | Pick one, finish or deliberately abandon the rest |
| Judge progress by purchases made | Judge progress by sessions actually completed |
| Long-form courses that reward starting, not finishing | Short daily formats built around consistent completion |
| Vague "I'll get to it" intentions | A specific daily time and trigger for the habit |

## Why Buying Feels Like Progress (Even When It Isn't)

Purchasing a course activates a real, if minor, sense of accomplishment and identity shift — "I'm the kind of person taking my career/skills seriously" — without requiring any of the actual work that identity implies. This is sometimes called the "buyer's high" in behavior research on aspirational purchases generally, and it's not unique to courses; it shows up with gym memberships, books, and equipment for hobbies too. The trap is that this feeling substitutes for real progress just convincingly enough that it can go unnoticed for a long time, especially because each individual purchase feels reasonable in isolation.

The uncomfortable industry-wide backdrop here is that most self-paced online courses are never finished by the people who buy them — completion rates for free MOOCs are commonly cited around 3-6%, and even paid courses see substantial drop-off. Serial course-buying is, in some sense, a rational (if costly) response to a format that most people don't finish anyway — if finishing is unlikely regardless, buying feels lower-stakes than it should.

## The Moratorium: A Practical First Step

The most direct fix is a temporary moratorium: no new course purchases until you've finished (or explicitly decided to abandon) everything you currently own. This isn't about permanent restriction — it's about breaking the automatic loop where buying substitutes for doing. During the moratorium, go back through your existing purchases and either commit to one with a specific plan, or consciously let the rest go without guilt. Guilt about unfinished courses is dead weight; deciding to abandon something on purpose is different from failing to finish it by default.

## Fix the Format, Not Just the Behavior

Part of why serial course-buying happens is that the format itself — long, front-loaded video libraries — makes starting easy and finishing hard, which means the buying moment feels satisfying while the actual completion moment rarely arrives to reinforce the habit. Switching to a format built around short daily sessions with visible progress (streaks, completion percentages you actually see) changes the reinforcement loop: you get a small, real sense of accomplishment from finishing something daily, not just from clicking "buy." See [why you never finish online courses](/blog/why-you-never-finish-online-courses) for the deeper mechanics of why long-form formats specifically struggle here.

## A Simple System Going Forward

- **One active learning commitment at a time.** Not five "someday" courses running in parallel.
- **A specific daily trigger.** Attach the habit to something already in your routine (morning coffee, commute) rather than relying on remembering to "get to it."
- **Track completions, not purchases.** Judge your learning progress by sessions actually finished this week, not courses added to your library.
