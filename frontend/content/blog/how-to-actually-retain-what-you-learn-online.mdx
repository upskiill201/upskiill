You finish a course, feel like you understood everything, and two weeks later you can barely explain what it covered. This isn't a sign you're bad at learning — it's what happens to almost everyone who learns passively, which is most online courses by default. [Teyro](/onboarding/0) was built around forcing active use of material specifically because passive video-watching has a terrible track record for retention.

## Direct Answer: What Actually Makes Learning Stick?

Retention comes from active use of information — recalling it without looking, applying it to a new situation, or explaining it to someone else — not from how many times you watch or reread it. Passive review (rewatching, rereading) feels productive but is one of the weakest retention methods available.

| Method | Retention Impact | Effort |
|---|---|---|
| Rewatching a video | Low | Low |
| Rereading notes | Low | Low |
| Active recall (testing yourself) | High | Medium |
| Explaining it to someone else | High | Medium |
| Applying it to a new problem | High | High |
| Spaced review over days/weeks | High (compounds) | Medium |

## Why Passive Learning Feels Productive But Isn't

There's a well-documented gap between how effective a learning method feels and how effective it actually is. Watching a video and nodding along feels like understanding, because the material is legible and easy to follow in the moment. But "easy to follow while someone else does the work" and "able to reproduce or apply it yourself later" are completely different skills, and online courses — built around video because it's easy to produce and scales infinitely — lean heavily on the format that produces the weaker result. This mismatch is arguably a bigger driver of the notoriously low online course completion and retention rates than laziness or lack of motivation.

### The Fix Is Active, Not More

The instinct when something didn't stick is to consume more content — rewatch the video, read a second article on the same topic. This rarely works, because the problem usually isn't insufficient exposure, it's insufficient active processing. The fix is almost always to do something with the information rather than absorb more of it:

- **Active recall** — close the material and try to write out or say what you just learned, then check what you missed.
- **Application** — use the concept on a real (even small) problem immediately, rather than waiting for a "someday" project.
- **Teaching it** — explain the concept out loud to someone, or even just to yourself, in your own words. If you can't explain it simply, you haven't fully retained it yet.
- **Spaced review** — revisit the material at increasing intervals (a day later, a week later, a month later) rather than only once.

## How to Structure Any Online Learning for Retention

1. **Never end a session on pure consumption.** If you watched a video, spend the last few minutes writing a summary from memory before checking it against the source.
2. **Build in a delay before moving on.** Immediately jumping to the next topic without any recall step is the single most common retention killer in self-paced online courses.
3. **Force yourself to apply, not just understand.** Understanding a concept and being able to use it are different states, and only the second one tends to survive more than a few days.
4. **Revisit deliberately, not accidentally.** Set a specific point to come back to earlier material rather than hoping a later module happens to reference it.
