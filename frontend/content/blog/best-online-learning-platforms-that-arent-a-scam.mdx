The online learning space has enough legitimate confusion (which platform is actually good for your goals) layered on top of a real scam problem (fake urgency, guaranteed-income claims, high-pressure funnels dressed up as "courses") that it's genuinely hard to tell the two apart from a landing page alone. This is a practical guide to separating them — what real red flags look like versus what's just normal marketing, and a general framework you can apply to any platform, including [Teyro](/onboarding/0), before trusting it with your time or money.

## Direct Answer: How to Spot a Legitimate Online Learning Platform

Legitimate online learning platforms have a working free tier or trial, transparent and easy-to-find pricing, real findable information about the team or company, and marketing claims that stay realistic rather than promising guaranteed outcomes or fast riches. Scam-adjacent platforms typically rely on urgency tactics, vague or hidden pricing until you're deep in a sales funnel, and claims that sound too good to be checked against reality.

| Signal | Legitimate Platform | Scam Warning Sign |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Free trial/tier | Available, no major catches | None, or requires payment info upfront with no real trial |
| Pricing | Clear, findable before signup | Hidden until deep in a funnel or sales call |
| Claims | Realistic, hedged ("can help you build X") | Guaranteed income, unrealistic timelines |
| Urgency tactics | Rare or absent | Constant countdown timers, "price goes up in 1 hour" |
| Team/company info | Findable, real | Anonymous, vague, or unverifiable |
| Reviews | Present on independent platforms, mixed is normal | Only glowing testimonials on their own site, nothing independent |

## Why This Space Has a Real Scam Problem

The online education boom created genuine opportunity, and it also created a profitable playbook for bad actors: promise a fast, guaranteed outcome (income, a new career, mastery), build urgency around a limited-time offer, and extract payment before the buyer has any real way to evaluate what they're getting. This isn't paranoia — it's a well-documented pattern across "make money online" and some course-selling ecosystems specifically, and it's fair for a skeptical buyer to screen for it.

The good news is the tells are fairly consistent once you know to look for them, and they're mostly about sales tactics, not subject matter — a legitimate coding course and a scam course can cover similar topics, but they behave very differently in how they sell themselves.

## The Practical Checklist Before You Pay Anything

**Is there a real way to try it first?** A genuinely confident platform lets you sample the actual product — a free lesson, a trial period, a demo — before asking for money. Platforms that skip straight to a sales page with no hands-on preview deserve extra scrutiny.

**Does the pricing show up before you've given your email and sat through a pitch?** Hidden pricing that only reveals itself after a "free masterclass" funnel or a sales call is a common structure in lower-quality or scam-adjacent offers, because it's designed to build commitment before you see the number.

**Are the outcome claims realistic?** "Learn practical coding skills through consistent daily practice" is a realistic claim. "Guaranteed six-figure income in 30 days" is not, regardless of the subject. Be skeptical of any specific income or outcome guarantee tied to a course purchase.

**Can you find real information about who's behind it?** A real company name, a findable team, and some kind of support contact matter. If a platform's "about" page is vague marketing copy with no verifiable specifics, that's worth noting.

**What do independent sources say?** Testimonials on the platform's own site are marketing. Reviews on independent platforms, app stores, or genuine forum discussion carry more weight, even when they're mixed rather than uniformly glowing.
