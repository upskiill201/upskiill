Somewhere along the way, "prove you can do it" quietly turned into "get a certificate," and the two aren't actually the same thing. A certificate proves you finished a course. It doesn't automatically prove you can do the thing the course was about — and most people evaluating your skills, whether employers or clients, know the difference even if they don't say it out loud. [Teyro](/onboarding/0) is built around the skill itself, not a badge at the end of it, because that's ultimately what holds up.

## Direct Answer: Usually No, With Real Exceptions

For most skills — coding, writing, business fundamentals, communication — you don't need a certificate to prove capability; a portfolio of real, applied work does that job more convincingly. The exception is fields with standardized, often legally or contractually required credentials (certain IT certifications, financial licenses, safety compliance), where a specific recognized certificate genuinely matters and can't be substituted with a portfolio alone.

| Field/situation | Does a certificate matter? |
|---|---|
| Freelance writing, design, general coding | Rarely required; portfolio dominates |
| Business/entrepreneurship skills | Rarely required; results and case studies matter more |
| Regulated IT/security certifications | Often genuinely required or strongly preferred |
| Financial licensing (certain roles) | Often legally required |
| Project management (certain employers) | Sometimes preferred, varies by employer |

## What a Certificate Actually Signals

A course completion certificate mostly tells someone: this person enrolled in and finished a structured program. That's not nothing — it suggests some baseline follow-through — but it doesn't verify competence the way a licensing exam or a piece of real work does. Most online course certificates aren't independently audited or standardized; they're issued by the platform itself, which means their credibility rests entirely on the platform's reputation, not on any external verification of what you can actually do.

This is different from something like a recognized security certification or a state-issued financial license, which typically involves an independent exam, ongoing requirements, and real consequences for misuse. Lumping those together with a generic "certificate of completion" from an online course conflates two very different kinds of credential, and it's worth being clear-eyed about which one you're actually getting.

## Why Demonstrated Work Usually Wins

For most practical skills, the person evaluating you — a hiring manager, a client, a collaborator — ultimately wants to know one thing: can you do this, reliably, under real conditions? A certificate answers a proxy question ("did you finish a course about this?"). A portfolio, a working project, a writing sample, or a case study answers the actual question directly. Given a choice between the two, most evaluators will weigh direct evidence far more heavily, and many will barely glance at a certificate if solid applied work is sitting right next to it.

This is a big part of why [Best Way to Prove You Learned a Skill Online](/blog/best-way-to-prove-you-learned-a-skill-online) argues for building proof continuously as you learn, rather than treating a certificate as the finish line. The certificate, if you get one, becomes a minor supporting detail rather than the main event.

## A Reasonable Way to Decide

If you're unsure whether to prioritize a certification path or a portfolio-first approach, a simple filter helps: does your specific field have a standardized, third-party-recognized credential that employers or regulators explicitly ask for? If yes, pursue it alongside your applied work — it may be a real requirement, not just a nice-to-have. If no (which covers most creative, business, and general technical skill areas), put your primary energy into building demonstrable work, and treat any certificates you happen to earn along the way as a bonus, not the goal.
