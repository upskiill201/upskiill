Revenue share is the number every course creator eventually has to confront, and it's more complicated than the headline percentage most platforms advertise. A platform can quote a generous-sounding split while structuring most actual sales through a lower promotional tier, or pay from a royalty pool that makes per-course earnings hard to predict. This post breaks down how revenue share actually works across the major models, so you can evaluate a platform by what you'd actually keep, not just the number on the marketing page — including where [Teyro's](/onboarding/0) Creator Studio fits into that picture.

## Direct Answer: How Course Revenue Share Compares

Revenue share depends heavily on the platform's model, not just its headline percentage. Marketplace platforms like Udemy often pay 37-50% to creators on promotional-priced sales (a large share of their volume), subscription platforms pay from a royalty pool based on engagement rather than a flat cut, and creator-favorable platforms typically offer 70% or higher in exchange for you bringing more of your own audience.

| Platform Type | Typical Creator Share | How You Get Paid | Traffic Source |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Udemy (marketplace) | Often 37-50% on promo sales | Per-sale, tiered by origin | Platform-driven, heavy discounting |
| Skillshare (subscription) | Royalty pool, engagement-based | Shared pool by watch minutes | Platform subscribers |
| Coursera (varies by program) | Varies, often lower for standalone creators | Per-sale or revenue-share agreements | Platform + institutional |
| Creator-favorable platforms (e.g. Teyro) | Often 70%+ | Per-sale, transparent split | Mostly creator-driven, some platform discovery |

## Why the Headline Percentage Isn't the Whole Story

The number that matters isn't the percentage in isolation — it's percentage multiplied by realistic sale volume and price. A platform offering a lower percentage but driving ten times the traffic can net you more actual income than a platform offering a higher percentage on sales you have to generate entirely yourself through your own marketing. This is the real trade-off underneath every revenue-share comparison: discovery traffic versus payout percentage.

Udemy's model illustrates this well — its marketplace runs on near-constant promotional pricing, and a large share of sales happen through discounted, third-party-driven traffic where the creator's cut drops significantly. Creators with no existing audience sometimes accept this because Udemy's search traffic still generates volume that would be hard to replicate alone. But creators who do have an audience, or who care about long-term brand and pricing control, often find the trade-off unfavorable once they've built any following at all.

## What "Creator-Favorable" Actually Means in Practice

A creator-favorable revenue share is typically paired with less built-in discovery traffic — the platform is betting that you'll bring engaged students yourself, and in exchange gives you a larger cut of each sale plus more control over pricing (no forced discount culture eroding your positioning). This tends to suit creators who already have some audience — a newsletter, a following in their field, past students — more than someone starting from zero visibility.

It's also worth weighing engagement and completion mechanics into the comparison, not just the percentage. A platform where students actually finish your course generates more reviews, referrals, and repeat buyers over time — which affects your real income more than a few percentage points on the split. ## How to Choose Based on Your Situation

- **No existing audience, need discovery:** A marketplace with heavier traffic (accepting a lower split) may get you initial sales and reviews faster.
- **Some existing audience, want better margins and pricing control:** A creator-favorable platform with a higher split and transparent pricing tends to net more per sale.
- **Prioritizing long-term reputation and completion rates:** Weigh the platform's engagement mechanics, not just the payout percentage — a course nobody finishes hurts your reviews regardless of what you were paid for the sale.
