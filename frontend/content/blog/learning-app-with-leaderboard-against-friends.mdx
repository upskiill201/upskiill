There's a reason Duolingo's weekly leagues get mentioned constantly when people talk about what makes the app sticky: a little friendly competition against people you actually know is one of the more reliable motivation hacks in gamified learning, and it works on adults exactly as well as it works on teenagers. [Teyro](/onboarding/0) uses the same underlying mechanic — XP and progress that can be compared against friends — because it consistently outperforms motivation that depends purely on internal discipline.

## Direct Answer: What Makes a Leaderboard Actually Work

A good leaderboard against friends resets periodically (usually weekly), groups people of similar activity levels together rather than pitting a beginner against a veteran forever, and ranks based on consistent effort (like XP earned from showing up) rather than raw skill level, so newcomers have a real shot at competing. Leaderboards tied to people you actually know outperform anonymous global ones because the social stakes feel real.

| Leaderboard design | Effect |
|---|---|
| Permanent, never resets | Early leaders pull away, discourages latecomers |
| Weekly reset / league brackets | Keeps competition close and renewable |
| Ranked by raw skill | Beginners can't compete, demotivating |
| Ranked by effort/XP | Beginners can win by simply showing up |
| Global, anonymous | Lower social stakes, easy to ignore |
| Against known friends | Higher stakes, harder to quietly disengage |

## Why Light Competition Beats Willpower for Most People

Competition works as a motivator because it converts an abstract, internal goal ("I should practice more") into something concrete and comparative ("I'm three XP behind Sam this week"). That comparison is a much sharper, more immediate signal than a vague sense of self-improvement, and it taps into a social instinct that most people respond to whether they'd describe themselves as competitive or not. This is a well-documented piece of why gamification techniques work broadly in education — covered in more depth in [Gamification in Education](/blog/gamification-in-education) — and leaderboards are one of the most direct applications of it.

The "against friends" part specifically matters more than people expect. A global leaderboard with strangers has essentially no social cost to ignoring — you don't know rank #4,982 and never will. A leaderboard with your actual friend group carries real, if mild, social stakes: falling to last place among people you know is a genuinely different feeling than falling to an anonymous rank in the thousands, and that difference in stakes is what drives consistent daily engagement rather than a one-time novelty check-in.

## Where Leaderboards Can Backfire

Not everyone responds well to competition, and it's worth being honest about the failure modes. Someone who falls significantly behind their friend group on a permanent, never-resetting leaderboard can end up demotivated rather than driven — the gap starts to feel unwinnable, and unwinnable competitions tend to get abandoned entirely rather than pursued. This is exactly why weekly resets and league-style brackets (grouping similarly active users together, promoting and demoting like a sports league) matter so much in good design: they keep the competition close enough to feel worth showing up for, week after week, rather than a permanent scoreboard that ossifies into "some people are just ahead."

If you know competition genuinely demotivates you rather than energizes you, a leaderboard-free streak-and-XP system without social comparison might suit you better — the goal is consistency, and for some people, comparison undermines rather than supports that.
