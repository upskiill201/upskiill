Ask someone what spaced repetition means and they'll say "Anki" and "flashcards" almost every time — which makes sense, since that's where the technique got popularized. But the underlying principle (review right before you'd otherwise forget) applies just as well to skills like coding patterns, negotiation techniques, or business frameworks, most of which don't fit neatly onto a flashcard. [Teyro](/onboarding/0) builds this kind of review into its lesson progression rather than treating it as a separate flashcard deck you have to maintain.

## Direct Answer: How Does Spaced Repetition Work for Skills?

For skills, spaced repetition means resurfacing earlier concepts or techniques inside later practice, timed to hit right as you'd start forgetting them — not a flat flashcard of "what is X." A coding platform might reintroduce a loop structure inside a later project; a communication course might have you re-apply an earlier framework in a new scenario. The mechanism is the same as flashcards; the format is applied practice instead of recall of a fact.

## Why Flashcard-Style Spaced Repetition Breaks Down for Skills

Flashcards work great for information that has a clean question-answer shape: a word and its translation, a fact and its date, a term and its definition. Most real-world skills don't decompose that cleanly. "How do you structure a difficult conversation with a direct report" isn't a flashcard — it's a judgment call that depends on context, and testing it as a simple recall prompt strips out exactly the part that makes it a skill rather than a fact. This is the core reason most spaced repetition tools stall out the moment you try to apply them beyond vocabulary and trivia.

### What Skill-Based Spaced Repetition Actually Looks Like

Instead of "recall this fact," skill-based spaced repetition looks more like: "apply this technique again, in a new situation, before you've fully forgotten how." A few concrete patterns:

- **Interleaved practice** — mixing older skills into newer exercises instead of practicing them in isolated blocks. If you learned a negotiation framework in week one, a week-four exercise deliberately requires using it again alongside newer material.
- **Progressive project reuse** — coding curricula that have you rebuild or extend an earlier project rather than always starting fresh, forcing you to recall earlier patterns in context.
- **Reflection prompts tied to timing** — asking you to explain or apply an earlier concept specifically at the point research suggests retention starts dropping, rather than only at the moment you first learned it.

This is the logic behind [Teyro's](/onboarding/0) four-phase lesson structure — Learn, Apply, Reflect, Deepen — where later lessons' Apply and Reflect phases are built to pull earlier concepts back in rather than treating each lesson as a sealed unit you never revisit.

## Building Your Own Skill-Based Spaced Repetition (If Your Course Doesn't Have It)

If you're working through a course or resource that doesn't build in review, you can approximate it yourself:

1. **Keep a running list of concepts you've learned**, dated by when you first encountered them.
2. **Schedule a deliberate revisit** roughly 1 day, 1 week, and 1 month after first learning something — the classic spaced intervals used in memory research.
3. **Make the revisit active, not passive.** Don't just reread notes — try to apply the concept to a new, slightly different scenario without looking anything up first.
4. **Combine with a flashcard tool for the genuinely factual parts** of a skill (terminology, syntax, definitions) while handling the applied parts through the interleaved-practice approach above.
