## 1. Replace Passive Re-Reading with Active Recall

Most students use low-utility study methods: re-reading chapters, highlighting notes, and watching lecture videos. These create an "illusion of competence"—you feel like you know it because the page is in front of you.

* **The Scientific Fix:** Close your book or IDE and write down everything you remember from memory. Active retrieval forces your brain to build stronger neural pathways.

## 2. Leverage Spaced Repetition Algorithms

Your brain is biologically wired to forget useless data. The Ebbinghaus Forgetting Curve shows that humans lose up to 70% of new information within 24 hours unless it is reviewed.

| Review Interval | Memory Retention Target | Action |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Day 1** | Immediate consolidation | 15-minute interactive quiz |
| **Day 3** | First recall challenge | Solve 3 practice problems |
| **Day 7** | Long-term transfer | Build a mini-project applying the concept |
| **Day 30** | Permanent mastery | Explain the concept to a peer |

## 3. The Feynman Technique (Teach It Simply)

Whenever you encounter an intimidating concept—whether in [how to learn business skills](/blog/how-to-learn-business-skills) or coding algorithms:

1. Write the concept name at the top of a blank sheet.
2. Explain it using simple words an 8-year-old could understand.
3. Identify where your explanation breaks down or uses jargon.
4. Return to your source material to refine your mental model.

---

## 4. Interleaving vs. Blocked Practice

Instead of practicing 50 math problems of the exact same type in a row ("blocked practice"), mix 3 different related problem types together ("interleaved practice"). Interleaving forces your brain to categorize *which* tool to apply, which is what real-world exams and jobs demand.
