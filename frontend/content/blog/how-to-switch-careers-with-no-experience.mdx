The hardest part of switching careers with no experience isn't learning the new skill — plenty of people manage that. It's that a resume with zero relevant history doesn't get read the same way as one with a job title attached, no matter how much you've actually learned. The fix is building something a hiring manager can look at directly, rather than hoping a list of completed courses speaks for itself. [Teyro](/onboarding/0) is built around that same idea: skills that show up as demonstrated ability, not just a certificate.

## Direct Answer: How Do You Switch Careers With No Experience?

Build tangible proof of the skill — a portfolio, a project, a work sample — before you're relying on a job title to vouch for you, since a resume alone won't carry the weight a title normally does. Combine that with targeted networking in the new field and a realistic timeline of several months of consistent effort, not weeks.

| Step | What It Looks Like | Why It Matters |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Build real skill | Structured practice, not just watching content | You need actual ability, not just exposure |
| 2. Create tangible proof | Portfolio, project, case study, writing samples | Employers can't verify skill from a resume alone |
| 3. Network intentionally | Informational interviews, communities in the target field | Referrals bypass resume filters that penalize career changers |
| 4. Reframe your existing experience | Transferable skills from your old field | You're not starting from zero, just from a different angle |
| 5. Target realistic entry points | Adjacent roles, smaller companies, contract work | Lower barrier than jumping straight to a senior role in the new field |

## Why "No Experience" Feels Like a Wall (and How It Actually Works)

Hiring processes are built to reduce risk quickly, and resume screening is a blunt instrument for that — a job title and years of experience are easy to scan for, while "actually knows how to do this" is not. When you have no experience in the target field, you're asking an employer to take on more evaluation risk than a resume screen is designed to absorb, which is why so many career changers get filtered out before a human ever really looks at their application. The way around this isn't fighting the filter — it's giving the employer something that doesn't need the filter at all: a work sample they can evaluate directly.

### Proof of Skill Beats Proof of Study

There's a meaningful difference between "I took a course on X" and "here's a thing I built using X." The first asks an employer to trust that the course actually taught you something and that you retained it — a trust most hiring managers, having seen plenty of unfinished courses, are reasonably skeptical of. The second removes the guesswork entirely. This is why building a portfolio, even a modest one, does more for a career switch than accumulating certificates. ## A Realistic Path

**1. Pick a target role, not just a target industry.** "I want to work in tech" is too vague to build a plan around. "I want to be a junior data analyst" gives you a specific skill list to work backward from.

**2. Identify the 3-5 skills that actually gate entry.** Every field has a handful of skills that show up in nearly every job posting for the role — start there instead of trying to learn everything adjacent to the field.

**3. Build one real project per core skill.** Not a tutorial follow-along — something with your own decisions in it, even if small. This becomes the portfolio piece that does the talking a resume can't.

**4. Use your old field as a differentiator, not a liability.** A career changer from teaching into UX design, for example, often has genuinely strong user-empathy instincts a straight-out-of-bootcamp candidate might lack. Name that explicitly rather than hiding your background.

**5. Network before you're job-hunting, not during.** Informational interviews and communities in the target field surface referrals and unlisted openings that never show up in a blind resume submission, which matters even more when your resume alone won't clear the first filter.

**6. Expect several months, not weeks.** Real skill-building, portfolio work, and networking all take sustained time. Rushing it usually means walking into interviews with proof that's too thin to hold up.
