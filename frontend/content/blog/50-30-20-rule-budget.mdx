The 50/30/20 rule is the most popular budget for beginners because it fits in one sentence. Here is how it works, what it looks like in real numbers, and when to change it.

## Direct answer: what the 50/30/20 rule is

The 50/30/20 rule divides your **take-home pay** into three parts: **50% for needs** (housing, food, transport, utilities, minimum debt payments), **30% for wants** (eating out, entertainment, subscriptions), and **20% for savings and extra debt payments**. It was popularised by Elizabeth Warren and Amelia Warren Tyagi in *All Your Worth*.

## The three buckets

### 50%: needs

Things you must pay to live and work: rent or mortgage, basic groceries, utilities, transport to work or study, insurance, minimum debt payments.

### 30%: wants

Things that improve life but that you could pause: eating out, streaming, hobbies, trips, upgrades.

### 20%: savings and debt

An emergency fund, long-term savings, and payments above the minimum on debt.

## Examples at different incomes

The same rule, using take-home pay each month:

| Take-home | Needs (50%) | Wants (30%) | Savings (20%) |
| ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| $1,500 | $750 | $450 | $300 |
| $2,000 | $1,000 | $600 | $400 |
| $3,000 | $1,500 | $900 | $600 |
| $4,000 | $2,000 | $1,200 | $800 |

## How to apply it in four steps

1. Find your take-home income for the month.
2. Multiply by 0.5, 0.3 and 0.2 to get your three targets.
3. Add up your real needs. If they are above the 50% target, note by how much.
4. Adjust the wants and savings buckets until the total equals your income.

## When 50/30/20 does not work

- **High housing costs.** If rent alone takes 50%, the rule is already broken. Try 60/20/20 or 70/20/10.
- **Low income.** A small income often means needs above 50%. Saving even 5 percent is a win.
- **Large debt.** You may want 30% or more going to debt for a while.
- **Irregular income.** Percentages of an unpredictable number are hard. Plan with your lowest month.

## Alternatives to try

- **70/20/10:** 70% living costs, 20% savings or debt, 10% giving or fun. Useful when needs are heavy.
- **Zero-based budgeting:** give every unit of income a job until income minus spending equals zero. This is the method behind most of the budgeting templates in our [monthly budget template](/blog/monthly-budget-template).
- **Envelope method:** put a limit for each category in cash or in a separate account. When an envelope is empty, spending in that category stops.
- **Pay yourself first:** move savings out the day you are paid and budget the rest.

## Which one should a beginner use?

Start with 50/30/20 for a month to see how your real life compares. If needs are well above 50%, switch to a split that matches reality. Then use zero-based tracking to make sure every unit has a job. If you are on a small income, [how to budget on a low income](/blog/how-to-budget-on-a-low-income) gives a more realistic starting plan.

## The bottom line

50/30/20 is a rule of thumb, not a law. Use it to see where you stand, then build a plan from your own numbers.
