Skipping straight to tool tutorials without the principles is the most common reason beginner design work looks "off" without the person knowing why.

## Why "Learning the Software" Isn't the Same as Learning Design

A common mistake: people think learning graphic design means learning Photoshop or Illustrator. But software is just the pen — knowing how to use every tool in Illustrator doesn't tell you when to use a serif vs. sans-serif font, why a layout feels cluttered, or how to build visual hierarchy that guides the eye. Those are principle-level decisions, and they're what separate amateur design from professional design regardless of software.

This is exactly the kind of content that benefits from short, structured, repeated practice rather than long tutorials. When a lesson shows you two versions of the same poster — one with proper hierarchy, one without — and asks you to identify which communicates better and why, you're building actual design judgment. That's a fundamentally different (and faster) way to learn than watching someone build a poster in Illustrator for 40 minutes.

Teyro structures this as Learn (the principle — say, contrast and hierarchy), Apply (choosing or building the better version in a scenario), Reflect (why it worked), and Deepen (more nuance for people who want it). It's a format built around judgment-building, which is the actual skill graphic design requires more than software memorization.

## The Core Principles Worth Learning First

1. **Typography basics** — font pairing, hierarchy, readability, when to use serif vs. sans-serif.
2. **Color theory** — complementary/analogous schemes, contrast, accessibility (contrast ratios matter for real-world design).
3. **Layout and composition** — grids, whitespace, alignment, visual flow.
4. **Visual hierarchy** — guiding the eye to what matters most, in what order.
5. **Brand consistency** — applying principles consistently across multiple pieces.

Once these are reasonably solid, tool fluency becomes much faster to pick up, because you already know what you're trying to produce — you're just learning which button does it.

Starting with a format built around daily short sessions addresses that failure point directly.

## A Realistic Learning Path

- **Week 1-2**: Learn typography, color theory, and layout basics in short daily sessions. Open Canva or Figma alongside this and just experiment — don't worry about making anything "good" yet.
- **Week 3**: Start recreating designs you admire (a poster, a social post, a book cover) to practice applying principles in a real tool.
- **Week 4+**: Take on a mock brief — design a fictional brand's logo, business card, and one social post, applying everything consistently.
- **Ongoing**: Share work for feedback. Design improves fastest with outside critique, which no app can fully substitute.
