You just spent weeks learning something — video editing, copywriting, basic web development, whatever it was — and now you're staring at the gap between "I can do this" and "someone is paying me to do this." That gap is smaller than it feels, but crossing it requires a specific, honest approach rather than either overselling yourself or waiting indefinitely to feel ready. [Teyro](/onboarding/0) is built to get you to the "can genuinely do this" point faster, but this post is about what comes right after.

## Direct Answer: The Fastest Realistic Path to First Income

Start with your existing network (friends, former colleagues, local businesses) rather than cold-pitching strangers, offer a modest but non-zero rate for your first 1-3 projects in exchange for real experience and a testimonial, then use that proof to raise rates and pitch further afield. Matching the scope of what you take on to your actual current skill level — not overselling, not underselling — matters more than any specific pricing formula.

| Step | What to do | Why it works |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Warm outreach | Ask your network first, not strangers | Existing trust shortcuts the credibility problem |
| 2. Small, real project | Take on a small, well-scoped piece of work | Builds a genuine portfolio example, not a hypothetical one |
| 3. Fair, modest pricing | Charge something, even if below market rate | Free work often attracts low-respect clients and undervalues your time |
| 4. Collect proof | Get a testimonial, case study, or before/after | This becomes your pitch material for the next client |
| 5. Expand and raise rates | Pitch a wider audience with proof in hand | Now you're pitching with evidence, not just claims |

## Why Your Network Beats Cold Pitching at First

Cold-pitching strangers with zero track record is genuinely hard — you're asking someone to take a risk on an unproven quantity, competing against people who already have testimonials and portfolios. Your existing network already trusts you as a person, which lowers the bar considerably. A former colleague, a friend's small business, or a family connection is far more likely to give you a real (if small) paid project than a stranger found through a cold DM, and that first real project is what you need most.

## Don't Work For Free, But Don't Overprice Either

There's a temptation to either do the first project entirely free (to "build a portfolio") or to charge full market rate immediately (to "value your work"). Both extremes tend to backfire. Free work often signals low value and attracts clients who don't respect deadlines or feedback boundaries, since there's no financial commitment on either side. Full market rate before you have any track record often just fails to close the deal. A modest, fair rate — clearly framed as an introductory or portfolio-building rate — usually gets you the project while still respecting your time.

### Being Honest About Your Experience Level Actually Helps

Counterintuitively, being upfront that you're relatively new to a skill but confident in your ability often builds more trust than pretending to have more experience than you do. Most clients for small projects aren't expecting a decade of expertise — they're expecting honesty, communication, and a finished product that meets what was promised. Overselling your experience and then underdelivering does far more damage to your reputation than an honest "I'm newer to this, here's my approach and here's a fair rate" ever would.

## Turning One Project Into a Repeatable Pipeline

The real unlock isn't the first project — it's what you do with it afterward. Ask for a specific testimonial (not just "great job," but something concrete: "delivered on time, understood exactly what we needed"). Turn the work itself into a portfolio piece, with a brief write-up of the problem and what you did. Then use that combination — testimonial plus portfolio piece — as the core of your pitch to the next, slightly bigger client. Each project should make the next pitch easier; if it isn't, something in your follow-up process needs adjusting.

## Handling the Awkward Early Conversations

A lot of people stall out not on the skill or the pricing, but on the actual conversation of asking someone to pay them for something they just learned. It helps to reframe this mentally: you're not asking for charity, you're offering a fair trade — real value delivered for a fair, transparent price, with both sides aware of your current experience level. Practicing this conversation out loud, even just with a friend playing the role of a skeptical client, makes the real version noticeably less nerve-wracking. Most of the discomfort comes from imagining worst-case reactions that, in practice, rarely happen — most people respond reasonably to an honest, confident pitch.

## What to Do When a Project Goes Wrong

Not every early project will go smoothly, and how you handle a mistake matters more for your reputation than the mistake itself. If you deliver something the client isn't happy with, resist the urge to get defensive — ask specifically what fell short, offer a genuine revision or partial refund if warranted, and treat it as data about what to clarify better upfront next time (usually it's a scope or expectation-setting issue, not a skill issue). Clients who see you handle a misstep professionally often become more loyal than if everything had gone perfectly the first time, because it demonstrates the reliability that actually matters more than raw skill level in the early stages of monetizing something new.
