Most "mobile-friendly" apps are really desktop courses with a responsive layout bolted on. [Teyro](/onboarding/0) and a handful of others were actually built mobile-first.

## Why "Mobile-Friendly" Isn't the Same as "Mobile-First"

A lot of learning platforms check the "works on mobile" box by making their video player responsive. That's not the same as designing the actual learning experience around how phones get used — in short bursts, often with interruptions, usually one-handed, frequently in noisy or public environments where a 45-minute lecture video isn't realistic. Mobile-first design means the lesson itself is chunked into something that survives a bus ride or a waiting room, not just that the video resizes correctly.

### What Actually Makes an App Work Well on a Phone

- **Session length under 15 minutes.** Long enough to make progress, short enough to fit a real gap in your day.
- **Bite-sized, interactive content — not just video.** Passive video-watching on a phone, especially in public, is a worse experience than active tap-through exercises.
- **Progress that's visible immediately.** A streak counter, an XP bar, a completed lesson checkmark — small feedback that makes the app worth opening again tomorrow.
- **Works without needing a second screen.** If the "mobile app" constantly nudges you to finish on desktop, it's not really a phone-first product.

[Teyro's](/onboarding/0) lesson structure — Learn → Apply → Reflect → Deepen — was built around this: each phase is short enough to complete on a commute or between meetings, with the gamified loop (streaks, XP, coins) giving you a reason to open it again the next day rather than losing momentum.

## What Phone Learning Is Genuinely Good At (and Not)

**Good for:** language practice, conceptual understanding, business and communication skills, habit-building, spaced repetition, daily consistency.

**Weaker for:** deep hands-on coding projects requiring a real IDE, design work needing precision tools, anything requiring sustained focus beyond 20-30 minutes at a stretch.

The practical answer for most people is to use phone-first apps for the daily habit and conceptual layer, and switch to a laptop when a skill demands hands-on production work — writing real code, editing video, building a spreadsheet model. Trying to force the latter onto a phone usually just creates friction that kills the habit you were trying to build.

## How to Actually Build the Habit

1. Put the app on your home screen, not buried in a folder — visibility drives usage more than people expect.
2. Attach it to an existing habit: right after you check messages in the morning, or during a specific commute leg.
3. Protect the streak, but don't let a broken streak become a reason to quit entirely — restart the next day.
4. Use notifications sparingly but don't disable them completely; a well-timed nudge is often the difference between a 2-day streak and a 20-day one.
