=== conversation-starters
title: 50 Conversation Starters for Any Situation
desc: Fifty conversation starters for work, parties, networking and new friends, plus how to follow up and keep a chat flowing naturally.
tags: conversation starters, good conversation starters, conversation starters for work, how to start a conversation, icebreaker questions
rel: how-to-make-small-talk, small-talk-topics
q: What is a good way to start a conversation? | Comment on a shared situation or ask an open question about the person or event.
q: What if the conversation dies? | Ask a follow-up about something they said or move to a new topic.
q: What should I avoid? | Intrusive personal questions, controversial topics early on and one-word interrogations.
---
The first line is easier with a few ready examples.

## Direct answer

Good conversation starters are open questions or comments about the shared situation: the event, the setting, the work or a recent experience. Listen to the answer and follow up with curiosity.

## At work

1. What are you working on at the moment?
2. How did you end up in this team?
3. What is the best thing you have read or watched lately?
4. What has been the highlight of your week?

## At events

5. What brought you here?
6. Have you been to one of these before?
7. What did you think of that talk?

## Anywhere

8. Any good recommendations for [food, shows, travel]?
9. What are you looking forward to this month?
10. What do you do to unwind?

## Keep it going

- Follow up with "What was that like?"
- Share something small about yourself.
- Remember names and details.

## The bottom line

Ask, listen, follow up.

=== open-ended-questions
title: Open-Ended Questions: Examples That Keep Conversations Going
desc: Open-ended questions invite more than yes or no. See examples for small talk, work and interviews, how to ask follow-ups and mistakes to avoid.
tags: open ended questions, open ended questions examples, closed vs open questions, how to ask good questions, conversation skills
rel: conversation-starters, active-listening-skills
q: What is an open-ended question? | A question that cannot be answered with just yes or no and invites explanation.
q: What are examples? | "What did you enjoy most?" and "How did you get into that?"
q: How do closed questions differ? | Closed questions have short answers, such as "Did you like it?".
---
The shape of your question decides the shape of the answer.

## Direct answer

Open-ended questions start with words like what, how, why or tell me about, and invite a fuller answer. Use them to start and deepen conversations. Follow up with "What was that like?" or "Say more".

## Closed vs open

| Closed | Open |
| :--- | :--- |
| Did you have a good weekend? | What was the best part of your weekend? |
| Do you like your job? | What do you enjoy most about your work? |
| Is it busy? | What has been keeping you busy lately? |

## Tips

1. Ask one question at a time.
2. Pause and listen.
3. Follow up on what they said.
4. Avoid "why" when it may sound accusatory. Try "what led to...".

## The bottom line

Open questions invite stories.

Introverts often excel at the part of conversation that matters: listening.

Introverts can handle small talk by preparing a few questions, arriving early when it is quieter, aiming for one or two good conversations rather than many, focusing on listening and allowing time to recharge afterwards.

2. **Arrive early.**
3. **Aim for one-to-one chats.**

Small talk is the doorway to deeper conversation, not the point itself.

=== how-to-end-a-conversation
title: How to End a Conversation Politely (With Phrases That Work)
desc: Ending a conversation gracefully is a social skill. Learn polite exit phrases for events, work and chats, plus how to leave without awkwardness.
tags: ending a conversation, how to end a conversation politely, exit phrases, leave a conversation, networking exit lines
rel: how-to-make-small-talk, conversation-starters
q: How do I end a conversation politely? | Thank them, mention what you enjoyed and give a reason or a next step, then leave.
q: How do I leave a conversation at a networking event? | Say it was nice to meet them, offer to connect and move on.
q: What if someone keeps talking? | Wait for a pause and use a clear, kind exit phrase.
---
A good ending makes the whole conversation feel better.

## Direct answer

End a conversation by signalling you are wrapping up, thanking them, saying what you enjoyed and giving a natural reason or next step. For example: "It was great talking to you. I am going to grab a drink, but let us stay in touch."

## Phrases

- "It has been great chatting. I am going to say hello to a few others."
- "Thanks for the advice. I will try that and let you know."
- "I have to head off, but I would love to continue this. Can I send you a message?"

## Tips

1. Time it at a natural pause.
2. Smile and use their name.
3. Offer a next step if relevant.
4. Do not apologise too much.

## The bottom line

Kind, clear and brief.

=== find-common-ground
title: How to Find Common Ground in Conversations
desc: Finding common ground builds connection fast. Learn to listen for clues, ask about interests, share small details and connect with different people.
tags: find common ground, common ground in conversation, shared interests, connecting with strangers, conversation skills
rel: rapport-building, open-ended-questions
q: How do I find common ground? | Ask open questions, listen for clues about interests and experiences and share a small related detail.
q: What if we have nothing in common? | Find shared situations, such as the event or workplace, or be curious about their world.
q: Is common ground only about interests? | No. Values, experiences and goals count too.
---
Connection starts with "me too".

## Direct answer

To find common ground, ask open questions, listen for clues, such as hobbies, places or experiences, and respond with a related detail of your own. Shared situations, like the event or workplace, are an easy starting point.

## Steps

1. Ask what they do or enjoy.
2. Notice anything you relate to.
3. Share a short, related detail.
4. Ask a follow-up.
5. If nothing matches, show curiosity.

## Examples

- "You mentioned running. I am training for a 10k. How did you start?"
- "We both moved here for work. What has surprised you most?"

## The bottom line

Be curious, and common ground often appears.

q: What does FORD stand for? | Family, Occupation, Recreation and Dreams.

q: What topics should I avoid? | Anything intrusive or sensitive until you know someone better.
---
A simple map for safe, friendly topics.

## Direct answer

The FORD method gives four topic areas for conversation: Family, Occupation, Recreation and Dreams. Ask open questions in each area, respect privacy and follow the other person's lead.

## Sample questions

| Area | Question |
| :--- | :--- |
| Family | Do you have any plans with family or friends this month? |
| Occupation | What do you enjoy most about your work? |
| Recreation | What do you do to unwind? |
| Dreams | Where would you love to travel next? |

## Cautions

- Skip family questions if you are unsure.
- Respect cultural norms.
- Do not interrogate.

## The bottom line

A framework for ideas, not a script.

---
A good icebreaker warms up the room without embarrassing anyone.

## Direct answer

An icebreaker is a quick activity at the start of a meeting that helps people connect. Choose low-pressure questions or short games, give people the option to pass and keep it brief.

## Ideas

**Quick questions**
- What was the best thing you ate this week?
- What is one thing you are looking forward to?
- Which skill would you love to learn?

**Activities**
- Two truths and a lie.
- One-word check-in.
- Show and tell: an object on your desk.

**Remote**
- Chat-box answer to a fun question.
- Share a photo of your view.

## Tips

1. Match the activity to the group.
2. Respect privacy.
3. Join in yourself.

## The bottom line

Short, light and optional.

=== remembering-names
title: How to Remember Names: 7 Techniques That Work
desc: Remembering names shows respect. Seven techniques: pay attention, repeat, associate, use it early, write it down and recover gracefully if you forget.
tags: remembering names, how to remember names, name memory tricks, remember someones name, networking tips
rel: conversation-starters, networking-event-introductions
q: Why is it hard to remember names? | We often do not pay full attention at introductions or do not rehearse the name.
q: What if I forget a name? | Be honest: "I am sorry, remind me of your name."
q: Do memory tricks work? | Many people find association and repetition helpful.
---
Names are the sweetest sound, as the saying goes, and the easiest to forget.

## Direct answer

To remember names, focus on the person when you meet, repeat the name back, use it in the conversation, link it to a feature or image and write it down afterwards. If you forget, simply ask again.

## 7 techniques

1. **Pay attention** when they say it.
2. **Repeat it:** "Nice to meet you, Chidi."
3. **Use it** once or twice in the chat.
4. **Associate** with an image or a person you know.
5. **Ask for the spelling** if unusual.
6. **Write it down** soon after.
7. **Review** before meeting again.

## If you forget

"I am sorry, I have forgotten your name. Could you remind me?" People appreciate honesty.

## The bottom line

Attention is the secret.

## Direct answer

To handle awkward silence, stay relaxed, treat short pauses as normal and restart with an open question, a follow-up on an earlier topic or a comment on the surroundings. If the conversation has run its course, end it kindly.

## Ways to restart

- "You mentioned earlier that you were travelling. Where to?"
- "How has your week been?"
- "What do you think of the venue?"
