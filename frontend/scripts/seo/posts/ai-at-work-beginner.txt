title: Data Privacy and AI at Work: What You Should Never Paste Into AI
desc: Know what not to share with AI tools at work: customer data, secrets, confidential documents. Learn the simple rules and what to check in your tool's policy.
tags: data privacy ai, ai data privacy at work, what not to paste into chatgpt, confidential data ai, ai policy
rel: ai-at-work-roadmap-for-beginners, how-is-ai-used-in-the-workplace
q: What should I never paste into an AI tool at work? | Customer personal data, passwords and keys, confidential contracts, unreleased financials and anything your policy forbids.
q: Do AI tools store my data? | It depends on the tool and plan. Read the privacy policy and ask your IT team about approved tools.
q: Can I use my personal account for work? | Check your company policy. Approved business tools often have stronger controls.
---
The easiest way to cause a data incident is to paste it into a chatbot.

## Direct answer

Do not paste personal data about customers or colleagues, passwords and keys, confidential contracts, unreleased results or anything your organisation classes as confidential into AI tools unless they are approved for that use. Learn your policy, use approved tools and anonymise what you can.

## A quick traffic light

| | Examples |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Green** | public information, your own draft text with no names |
| **Amber** | internal documents: only in approved tools |
| **Red** | personal data, secrets, contracts, health or financial records |

## How to reduce risk

1. **Check the policy** and ask which tools are approved.
2. **Remove names and identifiers** before pasting.
3. **Use enterprise or business plans** with data controls when provided.
4. **Turn off training on your data** where the setting exists.
5. **Never paste credentials.**
6. **Ask when unsure.**

## The bottom line

If you would not email it to a stranger, do not paste it into an unapproved tool.

## Direct answer

To write reports faster with AI, start with the purpose and audience, ask for an outline, supply your own facts and numbers, draft section by section, then edit for accuracy, tone and clarity. Never let AI invent data, and check every figure.

## Workflow

1. **Define:** purpose, audience, length, decision needed.
2. **Outline:** "Suggest an outline for a one-page progress report."
3. **Supply facts:** paste your numbers and notes.
4. **Draft:** one section at a time.
5. **Edit:** simplify, cut and add your voice.
6. **Check:** numbers, names, dates and claims.

## Prompt example

"Using only the facts below, write a 150-word summary for senior managers. If something is missing, say so. Facts: ..."

## The bottom line

Let AI draft, let you decide what is true.

=== responsible-ai-use-at-work
title: Responsible AI Use at Work: Principles and Habits That Keep You Safe

tags: responsible ai use, responsible ai at work, ai ethics at work, ai accountability, ai guidelines for employees
rel: data-privacy-and-ai-at-work, ai-bias-explained
q: What does responsible AI use mean? | Using AI in ways that are safe, fair, transparent and accountable, with human oversight.
q: Who is responsible for AI-generated work? | You are. Whatever you submit under your name is yours to check.
q: Should I tell people I used AI? | Follow your organisation's guidance. Being open builds trust, especially for important work.
---
AI is a tool. You are accountable for how you use it.

## Direct answer

Responsible AI use means protecting data, checking outputs, watching for bias, being open about when AI helped and keeping a human accountable for decisions. Follow your organisation's policy, use approved tools and never let AI make high-stakes decisions about people alone.

## Seven habits

1. **Know the policy.**
2. **Protect data.**
3. **Check facts and sources.**
4. **Watch for bias,** especially in decisions about people.
5. **Be transparent** about AI's role where it matters.
6. **Keep a human in charge** of important decisions.
7. **Keep learning,** since tools and rules change.

## High-stakes uses

Hiring, performance reviews, medical, legal and financial decisions need extra care, human review and often specific rules.

## The bottom line

Use AI to help you do better work, not to avoid responsibility.

=== ai-bias-explained
title: AI Bias Explained: Where It Comes From and Why It Matters at Work
desc: AI can reflect and amplify bias in its training data. Learn how bias appears, examples in hiring and customer service, and how to test and reduce it.
tags: ai bias, what is ai bias, bias in ai hiring, algorithmic bias examples, fairness in ai
rel: responsible-ai-use-at-work
q: What is AI bias? | Systematic unfairness in AI outputs that arises from biased data, design choices or how a system is used.
q: Where does it come from? | Historical data that reflects past inequalities, unrepresentative samples and flawed assumptions.
q: How can I reduce it? | Test with diverse examples, keep humans in decisions about people and follow fairness guidance.
---
AI learns from human data. Human data contains human bias.

## Direct answer

AI bias is unfair or skewed output caused by biased training data, design choices or use. It matters at work when AI influences decisions about people, such as hiring or performance. Test with diverse examples, keep human oversight and follow your organisation's and local legal guidance.

## Examples

- A résumé screener that favours patterns from past hires.
- A voice tool that works worse for some accents.
- Image tools that reinforce stereotypes.

## What to do

1. **Do not use AI alone** for decisions about people.
2. **Test it** with varied inputs.
3. **Check results by group** where appropriate and lawful.
4. **Document** how it is used.
5. **Report issues.**

## The bottom line

Bias is a design and oversight problem, not just a technical one.

q: Can AI summarise a long document? | Yes, but long documents may need to be split, and summaries can omit or distort details.

q: Should I use AI for contracts? | Only as a helper. Have a qualified person review legal and medical documents.
---
Summaries save time when they are faithful.

## Direct answer

To summarise a long document with AI, say who the summary is for and what length you need, ask for key points, numbers and risks, split very long documents into sections and check the summary against the original. Do not rely on AI alone for legal, medical or financial documents.

## A prompt that works

"Summarise this document for a busy manager in five bullets, then list any numbers, deadlines and risks. Quote the sentence each bullet comes from."

## Tips

- Ask for quotes so you can verify.
- Chunk long text and combine the summaries.
- Ask "what is missing?" and "what is uncertain?"
- Keep confidential documents out of unapproved tools.

## The bottom line

Faster reading, with a final check by a human.
