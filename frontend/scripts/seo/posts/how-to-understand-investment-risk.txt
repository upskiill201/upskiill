title: Risk vs Return: How Higher Rewards Come With Higher Risk

tags: risk vs return, risk and return, risk return tradeoff, what is risk return tradeoff, investing risk
rel: what-is-investment-risk, investment-risk-tolerance
q: What is the risk-return trade-off? | The idea that higher potential returns usually require accepting higher risk of loss.
q: Does more risk always mean more return? | No. Risk means a wider range of possible outcomes, not a guaranteed higher return.
q: How do I choose? | Consider your goals, time horizon, ability to handle losses and need for the money.
---
There is no free lunch in investing.

## Direct answer

Risk and return are linked. Investments with higher possible returns generally carry greater risk of losing money or of big swings in value. Lower-risk options like cash tend to grow more slowly. The right balance depends on your goals, timeframe and comfort with losses.

## A simple spectrum

| Lower risk, lower expected return | Higher risk, higher expected return |
| :--- | :--- |
| Cash and savings accounts | Individual company shares |
| Government bonds | Emerging market investments |
| Short-term deposits | Speculative assets |

## How to choose

1. **Define the goal** and when you need the money.
2. **Consider how you would feel** if the value dropped 20 percent.
3. **Match risk to time:** money needed soon belongs in lower-risk places.
4. **Diversify** to avoid concentrated risk.

## The bottom line

Take only as much risk as you need and can handle. This is general education, not advice.

---
Prices move. Knowing that in advance makes it easier to cope.

## Direct answer

Volatility describes how widely and quickly an investment's price changes. High volatility means large swings in both directions. It is a normal part of markets. You can manage it by investing for the long term, diversifying and not holding money you need soon in volatile assets.

## What causes it

- News about the economy or companies.
- Interest rate changes.
- Investor mood and uncertainty.

## How to cope

1. **Match investments to your timeline.**
2. **Diversify.**
3. **Avoid checking prices constantly.**
4. **Stick to your plan** unless your circumstances change.
5. **Keep an emergency fund** so you do not sell in a downturn.

## The bottom line

Volatility is the price of potentially higher returns. This is general education, not advice.

---
How you split your money often matters more than which individual investments you pick.

## Direct answer

Asset allocation is the mix of asset classes in your portfolio, such as shares, bonds and cash. A higher share of shares generally means higher potential growth and more volatility. A higher share of bonds and cash means more stability. Your mix should fit your goals, timeline and comfort with risk.

## Factors that shape it

- **Time horizon:** longer horizons can ride out more volatility.
- **Risk tolerance:** how you feel about losses.
- **Risk capacity:** how much loss you can afford.
- **Goals:** growth, income or preservation.

## A gradual shift

Some investors move toward safer assets as they approach a goal. Some funds automate this.

## The bottom line

Choose a mix you can stick with, and review it occasionally. This is general education, not advice.

## Direct answer

A stock represents part ownership in a company, with returns coming from price growth and dividends. A bond is a loan you make to a government or company, which pays interest and returns the original amount at maturity. Stocks generally offer higher potential returns with more volatility. Bonds are usually steadier but not risk-free.

## Comparison

| | Stocks | Bonds |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| What you get | Ownership | Loan repayment plus interest |
| Typical return source | Price growth, dividends | Interest |
| Typical volatility | Higher | Lower |
| Main risks | Market and company risk | Credit, interest rate and inflation risk |

## How they work together

When stocks fall, bonds sometimes hold up better, which can smooth results, though this is not guaranteed.

## The bottom line

Understand both, then choose a mix that fits you. This is general education, not advice.

rel: risk-vs-return, what-is-investment-risk
q: What is liquidity risk? | It is the risk that you cannot sell an asset quickly at a fair price when you need the cash.
q: What are examples of illiquid assets? | Property, private business stakes and some collectibles.
q: How can I reduce liquidity risk? | Keep an emergency fund in cash and avoid putting short-term needs into hard-to-sell assets.
---
Having wealth on paper is not the same as having cash.

## Direct answer

Liquidity risk is the risk that you cannot turn an asset into cash quickly without losing value. Cash is highly liquid. Property and private investments are less so. Keep money you may need soon in liquid accounts and be wary of products that lock your money away.

## Examples

- Selling a house can take months.
- Some funds restrict withdrawals during stress.
- Collectibles can be hard to sell at a fair price.

## How to plan

1. **Hold an emergency fund** in easy-access savings.
2. **Match investments to timelines.**
3. **Check withdrawal rules** and fees before investing.
4. **Do not rely on illiquid assets** for short-term needs.

## The bottom line

Always ask: "How quickly can I get my money back, and at what cost?" This is general education, not advice.

## Direct answer

A market crash is a sharp, sudden drop in prices. If you have a long time horizon and a plan, many experts suggest avoiding panic selling, keeping your emergency fund separate and continuing regular contributions if you can. If you need the money soon, your plan should have protected it already.

## What to do

1. **Pause before acting.**
2. **Check your plan and timeline.**
3. **Do not sell in a panic.**
4. **Keep contributing** if your finances allow.
5. **Avoid borrowing to invest.**
6. **Speak to a qualified adviser** if you are unsure.

## What not to do

- Check prices every hour.
- Follow social media tips to "buy the dip" without a plan.
- Pull money you need for essentials.

## The bottom line

Prepare before a crash, keep a cool head during one. This is general education, not advice.

q: What is portfolio rebalancing? | It is adjusting your holdings back to your target asset allocation after market moves have shifted it.

q: How do I rebalance without selling? | Direct new contributions toward the asset class that is below target.
---
If shares rise, your portfolio quietly becomes riskier than you planned.

## Direct answer

Rebalancing means adjusting your investments back to your target mix after price changes have moved it. You can do it on a schedule, such as once a year, or when an asset drifts beyond a set range. You can also rebalance by directing new money to the underweight asset.

## Example

You targeted 60 percent shares and 40 percent bonds. After a strong year, shares are 70 percent. Rebalancing means shifting some back toward bonds.

## Approaches

- **Time-based:** review yearly.
- **Threshold-based:** act when a holding drifts a set amount.
- **Cash-flow:** use new contributions.

## Watch for

- Trading costs and taxes.
- Doing it so often that costs outweigh benefits.

## The bottom line

Rebalancing keeps your risk where you chose it. This is general education, not advice.

=== standard-deviation-investing
title: Standard Deviation in Investing: A Plain-English Guide
desc: Standard deviation measures how widely an investment's returns vary. Learn what it tells you about risk, an example and its limits.
tags: standard deviation investing, standard deviation, what is standard deviation in finance, measuring investment risk, volatility measure
rel: volatility-explained, risk-vs-return
q: What is standard deviation in investing? | It is a statistic that measures how much returns vary from their average, used as a measure of volatility.
q: Does a high standard deviation mean high risk? | It means returns have varied widely, which is one kind of risk, but it does not tell you whether losses are permanent.
q: Is it enough to judge an investment? | No. Use it alongside other measures and your own goals.
---
A number that describes how bumpy the ride has been.

## Direct answer

Standard deviation shows how far an investment's returns have typically moved from their average. A higher number means bigger swings. It is a common measure of volatility, but it does not predict the future or capture every type of risk.

## Simple example

Fund A returns between 4 and 6 percent each year. Fund B returns between -10 and 20 percent. Fund B has a much higher standard deviation.

## Uses and limits

**Uses:** compare how volatile investments have been.
**Limits:** it is based on past data, treats up and down moves equally and ignores the risk of rare extreme events.

## The bottom line

Use it as one tool among several. This is general education, not advice.

=== risk-capacity-vs-tolerance
title: Risk Capacity vs Risk Tolerance: What's the Difference?
desc: Risk tolerance is how you feel about losses. Risk capacity is how much loss you can afford. Learn why you need both before choosing investments.
tags: risk capacity, risk capacity vs risk tolerance, risk tolerance, how much risk can I afford, investing risk profile
rel: investment-risk-tolerance, risk-vs-return
q: What is risk capacity? | It is your financial ability to take losses without damaging your goals or lifestyle.
q: What is risk tolerance? | It is your emotional comfort with the possibility of losses and swings.
q: Which matters more? | Both. Choose investments that respect the lower of your capacity and tolerance.
---
You might feel fine about risk and still be unable to afford it.

## Direct answer

Risk tolerance is how comfortable you feel with investment losses. Risk capacity is how much loss you could afford financially. A secure income, a long time horizon and a large emergency fund raise capacity. When the two differ, choose investments that fit the lower one.

## Factors that affect capacity

- Income stability
- Time horizon
- Emergency fund size
- Debts and dependants
- How soon you need the money

## A quick example

Someone who feels fearless about risk but has no emergency fund and big debts has low capacity. Someone with plenty of savings who panics at small losses has low tolerance.

## The bottom line

Respect both. This is general education, not advice.
