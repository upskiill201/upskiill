=== opportunity-cost
title: Opportunity Cost: What It Means With Everyday Examples
desc: Opportunity cost is what you give up when you choose one option over another. See simple everyday money examples and how to use the idea to spend better.
tags: opportunity cost, opportunity cost meaning, opportunity cost examples, what is opportunity cost, spending decisions
rel: needs-vs-wants-examples, how-to-stop-overspending
q: What is opportunity cost? | It is the value of the next best alternative you give up when you make a choice.
q: What is an example of opportunity cost? | Spending 100 on a gadget means you cannot use that 100 for savings, a course or a trip.
q: Does opportunity cost only apply to money? | No. Time and energy have opportunity costs too.
---
Every purchase is also a decision not to buy something else.

## Direct answer

Opportunity cost is the value of what you give up when you choose one option. If you spend 100 on a night out, the opportunity cost is the next best use of that 100, such as saving it, paying off debt or buying something else. It helps you compare choices honestly.

## Everyday examples

- Buying a coffee every day instead of putting that money toward a goal.
- Taking a job with a longer commute, which costs time as well as money.
- Spending a weekend on a project instead of resting.
- Buying a new phone instead of repairing the old one and using the difference elsewhere.

## How to use it

1. Before buying, ask "What else could this money do?"
2. Compare the two options side by side.
3. Include time, not just price.
4. Choose what fits your priorities.

## Not every choice should be a sacrifice

The goal is not to say no to everything. It is to say yes to what matters most.

## The bottom line

Price tells you what you pay. Opportunity cost tells you what it really costs.

=== lifestyle-inflation
title: Lifestyle Inflation: How It Quietly Eats Your Raise
desc: Lifestyle inflation is spending more as you earn more. Learn how it happens, why it keeps people stuck and simple rules to keep more of every raise.
tags: lifestyle inflation, lifestyle creep, what is lifestyle inflation, how to avoid lifestyle inflation, spending more as you earn more
rel: lifestyle-creep, money-habits-that-build-wealth
q: What is lifestyle inflation? | It is the habit of increasing your spending as your income rises, so that a raise does not improve your savings.
q: How do I avoid lifestyle inflation? | Decide in advance how much of each raise goes to savings, automate it and upgrade only the things you value.
q: Is lifestyle inflation always bad? | No. Some improvements are worth it. The problem is when spending rises automatically with income.
---
A raise should improve your life. If it just raises your bills, it does not.

## Direct answer

Lifestyle inflation means your spending grows as your income grows, so you never get ahead. A higher salary leads to a bigger home, nicer car and more subscriptions, and savings stay flat. To avoid it, set aside part of each raise before you start spending the rest.

## How it happens

- Small upgrades that feel normal: better food, newer gadgets.
- Spending to match friends or coworkers.
- Treating a raise as "extra money" to spend.

## A simple rule

When your income rises, split the increase. For example, save half and use half to enjoy. Automate the savings part on the same day your first pay rise lands.

## Spend on what you value

Upgrades are fine when they add real value. Ask: "Would I choose this if I had to save for it?"

## Related

See [lifestyle creep](/blog/lifestyle-creep) for more on the same idea.

## The bottom line

Keep your lifestyle growing slower than your income.

=== hedonic-adaptation
title: Hedonic Adaptation: Why New Things Stop Making You Happy
desc: Hedonic adaptation is getting used to new purchases so the joy fades. Learn how it works and how to spend on things that give lasting satisfaction.
tags: hedonic adaptation, hedonic treadmill, why new things stop making us happy, spending and happiness, buying happiness
rel: needs-vs-wants-examples, opportunity-cost
q: What is hedonic adaptation? | It is the tendency to return to a baseline level of happiness after positive or negative changes, so new things feel less exciting over time.
q: What is the hedonic treadmill? | It describes chasing new purchases or achievements to feel happy, then adapting and wanting more.
q: What spending lasts longer in happiness? | Research often suggests experiences and time with others tend to give longer-lasting satisfaction than possessions, though people differ.
---
The new phone thrilled you for a week. By month two it was just a phone.

## Direct answer

Hedonic adaptation is the way we get used to new things so the happiness they bring fades. A new car, outfit or gadget feels exciting, then becomes normal. Knowing this helps you spend on what lasts: experiences, relationships and things you use often.

## Why it matters for money

If every purchase fades, spending more does not keep you happier. You can end up on a treadmill, buying again to get the same buzz.

## How to slow it down

1. **Choose experiences or tools you use often.**
2. **Space out upgrades.**
3. **Practise gratitude** for what you already have.
4. **Share purchases** with others, such as a trip or a meal.
5. **Add variety,** rather than more of the same.

## A useful question

"How often will I use this, and will I still care in three months?"

## The bottom line

Buy fewer things that excite for a week and more that help for years.

tags: value based spending, values based budgeting, spend on what matters, spending priorities, conscious spending plan
rel: needs-vs-wants-examples, conscious-spending
q: What is value-based spending? | It is choosing where to spend according to what matters most to you, and cutting spending that does not.
q: How do I find my spending values? | List what you enjoyed most or regretted least in the past year, then look for patterns.
q: Can value-based spending save money? | Often yes, because it highlights spending that does not match your priorities.
---
Two people on the same income can spend very differently. Values explain why.

## Direct answer

Value-based spending means directing your money to what you care about most, such as family, learning, travel or health, and cutting spending that does not add value. You start by naming your values, then check each spending category against them.

## Step by step

1. **List your top three to five values.**
2. **Review last month's spending** and mark each item as high, medium or low value.
3. **Cut or reduce low-value items.**
4. **Give high-value categories a proper budget.**
5. **Review every quarter** as your life changes.

## Example

Someone who values learning might spend more on courses and books and less on clothing. Someone who values time with family might choose a smaller home near relatives over a bigger one farther away.

## The bottom line

Spend lavishly on what you love and ruthlessly less on what you do not.

=== sunk-cost-fallacy
title: Sunk Cost Fallacy: What It Is and How It Costs You Money
desc: The sunk cost fallacy is continuing with something because of what you already spent. Learn the money examples and how to decide with future value in mind.
tags: sunk cost fallacy, sunk cost, sunk cost examples, sunk cost fallacy in money, decision making
rel: opportunity-cost, buyers-remorse
q: What is the sunk cost fallacy? | It is the mistake of continuing something because you have already invested time or money, even when stopping would be better.
q: What is an example of a sunk cost? | Finishing an expensive meal you do not enjoy, or keeping a gym membership you never use because you already paid.
q: How do I avoid it? | Ask, "If I had not already spent this, would I choose it now?"
---
Money already spent is gone. Only the next decision is yours.

## Direct answer

The sunk cost fallacy is staying with a choice because of what you have already spent, even when it no longer makes sense. The money or time cannot be recovered whatever you decide, so the sensible question is what gives the best result from now on.

## Everyday examples

- Eating food you dislike because you paid for it.
- Keeping a gym membership you never use.
- Repairing an old car again and again because of past repair bills.
- Staying in a course you hate because you paid the fee.

## How to break the habit

1. **Ask the fresh-start question:** "If I were deciding today with no past spending, would I do this?"
2. **Compare future costs and benefits only.**
3. **Accept the loss** and move on.
4. **Set stop points** before starting big projects.

## The bottom line

You cannot get the past money back, but you can protect the future money.

=== delayed-gratification
title: Delayed Gratification: How to Wait for Bigger Rewards
desc: Delayed gratification means resisting a small reward now for a bigger one later. Learn how it helps you save and simple ways to build the habit.
tags: delayed gratification, delayed gratification meaning, delayed gratification examples, self control and money, marshmallow test
rel: wish-list-method, money-habits-that-build-wealth
q: What is delayed gratification? | It is choosing to wait for a larger or better reward instead of taking a smaller one immediately.
q: Why is delayed gratification important for money? | Saving, investing and paying off debt all require giving up spending now for benefit later.
q: Can you get better at it? | Yes. Habits, clear goals and removing temptations make it easier.
---
Saving is delayed gratification in action.

## Direct answer

Delayed gratification means waiting for a bigger reward rather than taking a smaller one now. In money terms, it is saving for a goal instead of spending today. You can build it with clear goals, automation, rewards along the way and by making temptations harder to reach.

## Techniques

1. **Set a vivid goal** and put it where you see it.
2. **Automate saving** so it happens before you spend.
3. **Use waiting rules** such as 24 hours or 30 days.
4. **Break goals into milestones** and celebrate each one.
5. **Remove triggers** like saved cards.

## A note on fairness

Waiting is easier with stable income. If money is tight, small rewards can matter. Plan some fun money so the process is sustainable.

## The bottom line

Pick goals that are worth waiting for and make waiting easier.

## Direct answer

Discretionary spending is money you spend on wants rather than needs, such as dining out, entertainment, hobbies, travel and new gadgets. Non-discretionary spending covers essentials like housing, utilities and basic food. Setting a limit on discretionary spending lets you enjoy it without guilt.

## Examples

| Discretionary | Non-discretionary |
| :--- | :--- |
| Takeaways and restaurants | Rent and utilities |
| Streaming services | Basic groceries |
| Hobbies | Transport to work |
| Holidays | Health costs |
| New clothes | Minimum debt payments |

## How to manage it

1. Total last month's wants.
2. Set a monthly limit that fits your goals.
3. Use a separate account or card for it.
4. Decide your top three treats and prioritise them.

## The bottom line

A fun budget with a limit is better than a vague guilt.

q: What are essential expenses? | They are the costs required for basic living and work, such as housing, utilities, food, transport, health and minimum debt payments.
q: Is internet an essential expense? | For many people it is, especially for work or study, though the plan level may be a want.
q: How do I separate needs from wants? | Ask whether you could function without it and whether a cheaper version would do.
---
You cannot budget well until you know what you cannot cut.

## Direct answer

Essential expenses are the costs you must pay to live and work: housing, utilities, basic food, transport, health care, insurance and minimum debt payments. Anything beyond the basic version, such as premium plans or dining out, is usually a want.

## Typical essentials

- Rent or mortgage
- Electricity, water, heating
- Basic groceries
- Transport to work or school
- Health costs and necessary insurance
- Childcare
- Minimum payments on debts
- Basic phone and internet

## Be honest about the level

Food is essential, but takeaways are not. A phone is essential, but a premium plan might not be. Split each category into "basic" and "extra".

## Build your list

1. Go through three months of statements.
2. Mark each item essential, partly essential or non-essential.
3. Total the essentials. That is your minimum monthly figure.

## The bottom line

Know your minimum, then decide how to use everything above it.

q: What are the first financial priorities? | Cover essentials, build a small emergency buffer, pay minimum debts, then tackle high-interest debt and save for goals.
q: Should I invest before paying off debt? | It depends on the interest rate and your situation. High-interest debt is often prioritised first.
q: Should I build an emergency fund first? | Many people start with a small starter fund, then build it up as they pay off debt.
---
With limited money, order matters.

## Direct answer

A common order is: cover essentials, build a small emergency fund, pay minimums on all debts, clear high-interest debt, build a fuller emergency fund, then save and invest for long-term goals while keeping some money for enjoyment. Adjust it to your situation.

## A simple sequence

1. **Essentials** such as housing, food, utilities and transport.
2. **A starter emergency fund** to avoid new debt from small shocks.
3. **Minimum payments** on all debts.
4. **High-interest debt,** using the [avalanche](/blog/debt-avalanche-method) or [snowball](/blog/debt-snowball-method) method.
5. **A fuller emergency fund.**
6. **Long-term saving and investing.**
7. **Other goals and fun.**

## Make it yours

Employer pension matching, a mortgage deposit or a tight job market may change your order. If in doubt, speak to a qualified adviser.

## The bottom line

Know what comes first, then keep going down the list.

q: What is buyer's remorse? | It is a feeling of regret or anxiety after buying something, often about the cost or whether it was the right choice.

q: How do I avoid it? | Wait before buying, compare options and ask whether you will still want it in a month.
---
The thrill fades and the doubt arrives.

## Direct answer

Buyer's remorse is regret after buying, often because the purchase was impulsive, costly or not what you expected. If it hits, check the return window, return or resell the item if you can, and learn from what triggered it. To prevent it, wait before big purchases and compare options.

## What to do right now

1. **Check the return policy** and deadlines.
2. **Return or resell** if you can.
3. **If you keep it,** use it and look for what you like about it.
4. **Note the trigger:** sale, stress, peer pressure.
5. **Forgive yourself.** Everyone makes these mistakes.

## How to prevent it

- Use a waiting rule for non-essentials.
- Read honest reviews.
- Set a budget in advance.
- Buy fewer, better things.

## The bottom line

Use regret as feedback for the next purchase, not as punishment.

=== fixed-expenses-explained
title: Fixed Expenses: Examples and How to Lower Them
desc: Fixed expenses are costs that stay the same each period. See common examples and practical ways to lower rent, insurance, phone and other locked-in bills.
tags: fixed expenses, fixed expenses examples, what are fixed expenses, how to lower fixed expenses, fixed costs budget
rel: fixed-vs-variable-expenses, how-to-reduce-bills
q: What are fixed expenses? | Fixed expenses are costs that stay about the same each month, such as rent, loan repayments and insurance.
q: Can fixed expenses be reduced? | Yes, usually less quickly than variable costs, through renegotiating, switching providers or changing housing or transport.
q: Are utilities fixed expenses? | Usually not. They vary with use. A fixed-rate plan can make them predictable.
---
Fixed costs set the floor of your budget. Lowering them is the biggest long-term win.

## Direct answer

Fixed expenses stay about the same every month: rent, loan payments, insurance, subscriptions with set prices. They are hard to change in the short term but lowering them gives lasting savings. Review contracts, compare providers and, when it makes sense, change big costs such as housing and transport.

## Common examples

- Rent or mortgage
- Car payments and insurance
- Phone and internet plans
- Student loans
- Gym or software subscriptions

## How to lower them

1. **Renegotiate or switch** insurance, phone and internet.
2. **Cancel unused subscriptions.**
3. **Refinance or consolidate** debt if it lowers the total cost. Check terms carefully.
4. **Consider housing changes,** such as a roommate or a cheaper area.
5. **Choose cheaper transport** where possible.

## When to do it

Review fixed costs when contracts renew and at least once a year.

## The bottom line

The bigger the fixed cost, the more it is worth reviewing.

## Direct answer

Conscious spending means you decide in advance how much goes to essentials, savings and investments, and guilt-free spending, and then you spend your fun money without worrying. You cut ruthlessly on things you do not value so you can spend on what you do.

## A simple structure

1. **Fixed costs:** housing, bills, debt minimums.
2. **Savings and investments:** automated.
3. **Guilt-free spending:** whatever remains, for things you love.

## Make it work

- Automate savings first.
- Pick two or three areas where you will happily spend more.
- Cut one or two areas you do not care about.
- Review every month.

## The bottom line
