=== price-anchoring
title: Price Anchoring: How Reference Prices Change What People Pay
desc: Price anchoring uses a reference price to shape how value is perceived. Learn how it works, ethical examples and when it backfires.
tags: price anchoring, anchoring pricing, reference price, pricing psychology, was and now price
rel: how-to-price-a-product, psychological-pricing
q: What is price anchoring? | Showing a reference price, such as a higher original price or premium option, so other prices seem better value.
q: Is it ethical? | Yes if the reference price is genuine. Fake "was" prices are misleading and may be illegal.
q: How can I use it? | Show a premium option alongside your standard one.
---
Prices are judged by comparison.

## Direct answer

Price anchoring presents a reference price that makes your price feel fair or attractive: a premium plan beside a standard one or a genuine previous price. It works because people judge value by comparison. Use true reference prices only.

## Examples

- Three plans where the middle one looks best next to a higher-priced option.
- A real earlier price shown crossed out.
- A "per day" price that frames cost as small.

## Cautions

- Fake discounts damage trust and may break consumer laws.
- Do not make the anchor absurd.

## The bottom line

Make comparison honest and helpful.

=== tiered-pricing-explained

tags: tiered pricing, pricing tiers, good better best pricing, pricing plans, pricing page design
rel: freemium-model-explained, price-anchoring
q: What is tiered pricing? | Offering multiple packages at different price points with different features or limits.
q: How many tiers should I have? | Often three, since it is easy to compare.
q: How do I decide what goes in each tier? | Group features by customer type and value.
---
Different customers, different needs, different prices.

## Direct answer

Tiered pricing offers packages, often three, targeting different customer groups. The basic tier covers entry needs, the middle serves most customers and the top tier serves power users. Make the differences clear and base them on customer value.

## Design steps

1. Identify customer segments.
2. List features and their value to each.
3. Group into tiers.
4. Name tiers clearly.
5. Highlight the recommended tier.
6. Test and adjust.

## Mistakes

- Too many options.
- Differences that are unclear.
- Tiers based on cost to you rather than value to customers.

## The bottom line

Make choosing easy.

=== markup-vs-margin
title: Markup vs Margin: The Difference and How to Calculate Each
desc: Markup is profit as a percentage of cost and margin is profit as a percentage of price. Learn the formulas, examples and how to avoid mixing them up.
tags: markup vs margin, markup vs profit margin, how to calculate markup, margin formula, gross margin
rel: how-to-price-a-product, break-even-point-explained
q: What is the difference between markup and margin? | Markup is profit divided by cost. Margin is profit divided by selling price.
q: How do I convert between them? | Margin equals markup divided by one plus markup. Use the formulas carefully.
q: Which should I use to set prices? | Many businesses think in margin, since it shows how much of each sale is profit.
---
Same numbers, different percentages.

## Direct answer

Markup is the profit as a percentage of cost: (price − cost) ÷ cost. Margin is the profit as a percentage of price: (price − cost) ÷ price. If an item costs 60 and sells for 100, markup is 66.7 percent and margin is 40 percent.

## Example

| | Value |
| :--- | :--- |
| Cost | 60 |
| Price | 100 |
| Profit | 40 |
| Markup | 40 ÷ 60 = 66.7% |
| Margin | 40 ÷ 100 = 40% |

## Setting a price from a target margin

Price = Cost ÷ (1 − margin). For a 40 percent margin on a cost of 60: 60 ÷ 0.6 = 100.

## The bottom line

Choose one and be consistent.

=== break-even-point-explained
title: Break-Even Point: How to Calculate It and Why It Matters
desc: The break-even point is where revenue equals costs. Learn the formula, an example and how to use it to set prices and targets.
tags: break even point, break even analysis, how to calculate break even, break even formula, pricing decisions
rel: how-to-price-a-product, markup-vs-margin
q: What is the break-even point? | The sales volume at which total revenue equals total costs, so profit is zero.
q: What is the formula? | Fixed costs divided by the price per unit minus the variable cost per unit.
q: How can I use it? | To see how many sales you need and how price changes affect that number.
---
How many do I need to sell before I make money?

## Direct answer

Break-even units = fixed costs ÷ (price per unit − variable cost per unit). If fixed costs are 3,000, price is 50 and variable cost is 20, the break-even is 3,000 ÷ 30 = 100 units.

## Example

| Item | Amount |
| :--- | :--- |
| Fixed costs per month | 3,000 |
| Price per unit | 50 |
| Variable cost per unit | 20 |
| Contribution per unit | 30 |
| Break-even | 100 units |

## Uses

- Test whether a price is realistic.
- Set sales targets.
- Compare scenarios.

## The bottom line

Know your break-even before you set your price.

=== price-elasticity-explained
title: Price Elasticity of Demand: What It Is and How to Use It
desc: Price elasticity shows how demand changes when price changes. Learn the formula, elastic vs inelastic goods and how small businesses can test it.
tags: price elasticity, price elasticity of demand, elastic vs inelastic, pricing strategy, demand and price
rel: how-to-price-a-product, psychological-pricing
q: What is price elasticity? | A measure of how much demand changes when the price changes.
q: What does elastic mean? | Demand changes a lot when price changes.
q: How can I estimate it? | Test price changes carefully and compare sales, or use past data.
---
If you raise the price, how many customers do you lose?

## Direct answer

Price elasticity of demand is the percentage change in quantity demanded divided by the percentage change in price. Elastic goods see big changes in demand when price moves. Inelastic goods, such as essentials, change less. Test price changes carefully to learn.

## Example

If you raise the price by 10 percent and sales fall by 5 percent, elasticity is −0.5, so demand is inelastic and revenue likely rises.

## How to use it

1. Test small changes.
2. Watch sales, not just price.
3. Consider different customer groups.

## The bottom line

Learn how sensitive your customers are before changing prices.

## Direct answer

Hourly pricing is simple and fair when scope is uncertain. Project pricing gives clients certainty and rewards your efficiency. Value-based pricing ties your price to the client's results. Choose by clarity of scope, risk and your experience.

## Comparison

| Model | Pros | Cons |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Hourly | simple, covers scope changes | rewards slowness |
| Project | predictable, rewards efficiency | risk of scope creep |
| Value-based | high earnings potential | needs trust and evidence |

1. Decide your target income.
2. Add business costs and taxes.
3. Divide by realistic billable hours.
4. Compare with the market.

## Tips

- Define scope in writing.
- Build a buffer for revisions.

## The bottom line

Pick the model that matches the work and revisit it as you grow.
