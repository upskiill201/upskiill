## The story

**Grace** (from the shared cast) is 44, from Manchester, and has spent 18 years as a ward nurse. She has just started her first tech job as a junior UX researcher at **Kinwell**, a small health-tech company in Manchester that builds an app for booking hospital appointments.

Grace can calm a frightened patient at 3 a.m. in ninety seconds. But put her in an office kitchen, a tech meetup or a video call full of 25-year-olds, and she freezes. When she's nervous she slips into what her son calls **Nurse Mode**: "And how are we feeling today? Any pain?" That's the course's running joke.

**The twist the course builds to:** Grace was already brilliant at small talk. Nurses ask open questions, notice small details, put strangers at ease and know how to leave a bedside kindly. She just never called it a skill. The research says the same about most of us: **you're better at this than you think** (see "What the research says").

The countdown runs through her first two months: **Week 1 → Week 8**. It ends at a big tech conference in Manchester, where Grace talks to a stranger who turns out to be **Ada**.

## What the research says (the course's backbone)

Every lesson stays practical, and these findings sit underneath it. They're worth putting in front of learners because they change behaviour: knowing that the fears are usually wrong makes people more willing to try.

| Finding | Source | Where it's used |
|---|---|---|
| Commuters asked to talk to a stranger had a more pleasant journey than those who sat alone, even though a separate group predicted the opposite. People assume others don't want to talk. | Epley & Schroeder (2014), "Mistakenly seeking solitude", *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General* | Ch1 L2, L8 |
| Chatting briefly with a barista (instead of being purely efficient) raised positive mood and sense of belonging. More chats with acquaintances ("weak ties") went with happier days. | Sandstrom & Dunn (2014), *Social Psychological and Personality Science*; *Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin* | Ch1 L3, Ch3 L8 |
| **The liking gap:** after a conversation, people underestimate how much the other person liked them. The gap is bigger in shy people. | Boothby, Cooney, Clark & Sandstrom (2018), *Psychological Science* | Ch1 L8, Ch5 |
| People worry that strangers won't enjoy talking to them and that they lack the skill, but conversations with strangers generally go better than expected. | Sandstrom & Boothby (2021), *Self and Identity* | Ch1 L8 |
| Asking more questions, especially **follow-up questions**, makes people like you more, because it shows you're listening. People don't predict this. | Huang, Yeomans, Brooks, Minson & Gino (2017), *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* | Ch2 L2 |
| Daily small talk at work raises positive feelings and end-of-day well-being, but it can also distract from focused work. | Methot, Rosado-Solomon, Downes & Gabriel (2021), *Academy of Management Journal* | Ch3 L8, L9 |
| Conversations almost never end when both people want them to. People can't tell when their partner wants to stop. | Mastroianni et al. (2021), *PNAS* | Ch4 L7 |
| Deeper conversations with strangers feel less awkward and more enjoyable than people expect. | Kardas, Kumar & Epley (2022), *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* | Ch5 L1–2 |
| People sending thanks underestimate how happy and surprised the recipient will be, and overestimate how awkward it will feel. | Kumar & Epley (2018), *Psychological Science* | Ch5 L8 |
| Small talk as "phatic communion": talk whose job is to bond, not to inform. | Malinowski (1923), essay in Ogden & Richards, *The Meaning of Meaning* | Ch1 L3 |
| The **next-in-line effect**: we recall least what was said just before our own turn. This is one reason names vanish during introductions. (Brenner tested it with words; applying it to names is an extension.) | Brenner (1973), *Journal of Verbal Learning and Verbal Behavior* | Ch1 L7 |

**Handled carefully:**
- **Cultural norms** (how long greetings last, how comfortable silence is, which topics are safe) vary a lot. Most of what's online about specific countries is popular writing, not research. Lessons describe differences as "in many places" with examples from the cast, and teach learners to *watch and match*, never stereotypes.
- **Popular tools** like FORD (family, occupation, recreation, dreams) are taught as handy memory aids, not as science.
- **Social anxiety:** practical and non-clinical. One Deepen points to professional help if fear of social situations is seriously affecting someone's life.
- **Safety:** talking to strangers happens in public, everyday settings. "No thanks" is always respected. Nothing in the course coaches anyone to push past a clear signal that the other person doesn't want to talk.

## Chapter 1 — The first hello (Week 1; lessons 1–5 are free)

**Story:** Grace's first week at Kinwell. On Monday morning, at the coffee machine, a colleague says hi and Grace replies, "And how are we feeling today?" Femi laughs so hard he spills his tea, and they're friends from that moment. Through the week Grace learns why small talk matters and how to open it. **Climax (lesson 10):** Femi dares her to say hello to five people she hasn't met before Friday's team drinks.

| # | Lesson | Story beat | Main Learn format |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Nurse Mode | The coffee machine, Monday 8:52. What should Grace say next? | Story (choice) |
| 2 | Is small talk pointless? | The commuter study: the conversation you dread is usually the one you'd enjoy | Myth or Fact |
| 3 | What small talk is really for | Bonding, not information: "phatic communion", the barista study, weak ties | Reading |
| 4 | Openers that work anywhere | Comment on the moment, then ask: the room, the occasion, the person | Card deck |
| 5 | **Checkpoint: the lift** | Twelve floors with the CEO and nothing to say | Mixed review |
| 6 | Do they want to chat? | Tap the signals: headphones, eye contact, short answers, open posture | Tap to explore |
| 7 | Names that stick | Why names vanish (the next-in-line effect) and how to keep them | Step-by-step |
| 8 | The voice in your head | The fears Grace has, and what the research says actually happens (the liking gap) | Chat with Tey |
| 9 | Closed doors, open doors | "Nice weather?" vs "What brought you to Kinwell?" | Before / After |
| 10 | **Unit Challenge: five hellos by Friday** | Femi's dare | Whole chapter, harder |

## Chapter 2 — Keep it going (Week 2)
*Story: Grace can start conversations now, but they die after nine seconds. Femi teaches her follow-up questions; Jayden teaches her what NOT to say to his friends' parents. Climax: the team lunch, where Grace keeps a table of six talking, including Priya, who barely speaks.*

1. The nine-second conversation (Story) · 2. The follow-up question (Step-by-step) · 3. Free information: catch the hooks (Tap to explore) · 4. Interview or conversation? Share something back (Before/After) · **5 Checkpoint: the canteen queue** · 6. Safe topics, and the FORD memory aid (Card deck) · 7. Listen like a nurse (Chat with Tey) · 8. Silence, oversharing and talking about yourself (Myth or Fact) · 9. Topics to avoid, and how to steer away kindly (Reading) · **10 Unit Challenge: the team lunch**

## Chapter 3 — Small talk at work (Week 4)
*Story: Grace's first month. The Monday "how was your weekend?", the five awkward minutes before every meeting, video calls with Arjun in Bengaluru, and her first one-to-one with Kinwell's CEO. Priya shows her small talk for introverts. Climax: Kinwell's monthly social, where Grace has to talk to the CEO, a new starter and a client.*

1. "How was your weekend?" beyond "Fine" (Before/After) · 2. The five minutes before the meeting starts (Story) · 3. Small talk on video calls (Chat with Tey) · 4. Small talk with your boss, and their boss (Step-by-step) · **5 Checkpoint: the first one-to-one** · 6. Small talk for introverts: Priya's way (Reading) · 7. Small talk around the world: watch and match (Myth or Fact) · 8. Weak ties: why the receptionist matters (Card deck) · 9. Chatty or focused? Ending kindly and getting back to work (Tap to explore) · **10 Unit Challenge: the monthly social**

## Chapter 4 — A room full of strangers (Week 6)
*Story: Grace's first tech meetup. On her first try she spends an hour by the snacks and leaves having spoken to one man, about the snacks. Femi coaches her for round two. Climax: the meetup, take two, where she joins a group, introduces two people and leaves with three new contacts.*

1. The snack table (Story) · 2. Walking into a room: the scan and the open group (Step-by-step) · 3. Joining a group already talking (Tap to explore) · 4. "So what do you do?" A career-changer's answer (Before/After) · **5 Checkpoint: the name badge** · 6. Introducing two people to each other (Chat with Tey) · 7. Leaving a conversation gracefully (Card deck) · 8. Networking myths (Myth or Fact) · 9. The follow-up message (Reading) · **10 Unit Challenge: the meetup, take two**

## Chapter 5 — From small talk to real talk (Week 8)
*Story: Grace now likes talking to strangers, which worries Jayden. Small talk turns into real conversations: a colleague's bad news, a story that lands, a joke that doesn't. Final Challenge: the Manchester tech conference. In the coffee queue Grace talks to a woman from Lagos, who turns out to be the next speaker: Ada. They leave as friends.*

1. Beyond the weather: deeper talk goes better than you think (Myth or Fact) · 2. The bridge question: from small to real (Step-by-step) · 3. Tell a 30-second story (Reading) · 4. Humour that works, and humour that doesn't (Chat with Tey) · **5 Checkpoint: Jayden's parents' evening** · 6. Awkward moments, and how to recover (Story with choice) · 7. Remembering people: details, notes and "how did it go?" (Tap to explore) · 8. The thank-you that people remember (Before/After) · 9. Your small-talk toolkit (Card deck) · **10 Final Challenge: the conference coffee queue**

Every chapter uses at least 6 Learn formats, and no two lessons in a row share a main format.
