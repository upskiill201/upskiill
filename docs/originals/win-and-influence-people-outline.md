# Win and Influence People — course outline

> Teyro Original. Category: **Communication**. Marked **HOT** in onboarding and Explore.
> Source: every rule, story and fact comes from Dale Carnegie's *How to Win Friends and Influence People* (1981 revised edition). Nothing is added from outside the book.
> Drafted 2026-11-03.

## 1. Title

**Win and Influence People**
Subtitle: *The 30 people rules: be liked, be trusted, be followed.*

Why this title (founder pick, 2026-11-03): it carries the promise everyone knows from the book — win people, then influence them — in our own wording, without copying the book's title.

## 2. What makes this course different

The earlier Originals follow one invented character through a story. This one doesn't. The book was never a story; it was a **laboratory**. Carnegie gave his class a rule, sent them out to try it that week, and had them come back and report. The course is built the same way.

| Book idea | Course mechanic |
|---|---|
| True stories, told with a cliffhanger ("What do you suppose Meade did?") | Every lesson **opens on a true story from the book and stops at the turn**. The learner predicts what happened before the reveal. |
| Each chapter ends on one rule in capitals | Every story lesson ends on **The Rule**, one line, plus *why it works* (the human-nature reason the book gives). |
| "Go out and test it, then come back and report" | **Field Mission** at the end of every lesson: one small real-world thing to do today. The next lesson opens with "Did you try it?" |
| Blank pages at the back: "Record your triumphs here" | **Triumph Journal**: missions that worked go into the learner's own journal (names, date, result, as the book asks). |
| The banker's Saturday-night review ("What mistakes did I make? What did I do right?") | **Weekly Review**: every chapter finale ends with the banker's three questions about the learner's own week. |
| "Offer a friend a dime every time they catch you breaking a rule" | **Catch Me** game in Deepen: spot the rule being broken in short everyday lines. |

### The three-lesson rhythm (every rule)

1. **Story** — a true story from the book, stopped at the cliffhanger; predict; reveal; The Rule; why it works.
2. **Practice** — the rule in today's situations (texts, meetings, family, customer service). Pick the reply, build the sentence, sort the lines, rewrite the scold.
3. **Pressure** — the hard version: you're angry, you're right, you're tired, it's your boss. The book's own counter-cases and warnings (flattery isn't appreciation; sympathy isn't lying).

Each chapter ends with a **Real Day** finale: a branching day where the learner needs all three of that chapter's rules, then the Weekly Review.

### Phases inside a lesson (the 4-phase model)

- **Learn** — `story` card (beats + a `choice` cliffhanger), then `callout` for The Rule, `reading` for why it works.
- **Apply** — 5–8 exercises: `mcq` (chat variant for "what do you reply?"), `tapBuild` (build the better sentence), `sortGroups` (honey vs gall, appreciation vs flattery), `matchPairs` (story ↔ rule), `typeAnswer` sparingly.
- **Reflect** — Field Mission + Triumph Journal prompt; from lesson 2 on, a check-in on the last mission.
- **Deepen** — one or two extra stories from the same book chapter as quick cases, plus Catch Me.

### Questions

Every question is anchored in the book: "What did Lincoln do with the letter?", "Why did the hotel manager drop the rent?", "Which rule did the troubleshooter use for three hours?". Practice scenarios are modern, but the right answer is always the book's rule, and wrong answers are the book's named mistakes (criticism, arguing, giving orders, flattery). Vary the correct option's position: the player does not shuffle.

## 3. Voice and tone — write it like Carnegie talks

The 1981 preface describes the voice as brash, breezy, exuberant, colloquial, conversational: he wrote as he spoke. Our copy must sound like that, in our own sentences.

**Do**
- **Talk to "you", and include yourself.** "You and I", "let's", "Did you ever stop to think of that?"
- **Open on a story, never on a definition.** Date, place, a name, a vivid detail, then the turn.
- **Ask, then answer.** "Was she unusual? No." "Absurd? Laughable? You are privileged to think so."
- **Repeat the big line on purpose.** "Let me repeat that." Carnegie repeats his key sentences; so do we, once per lesson.
- **Short punches after long build-ups.** "He was right, dead right." "It pays." "There is no other way."
- **Plain, warm, a little old-fashioned in rhythm,** but modern in setting. Texts, Slack, a landlord, a group chat: fine. Slang of today: no.
- **Confess.** Carnegie admits his own blunders (the Shakespeare argument, the "Dictated but not read" letter). Our narrator can say "I've done it too."
- **Everything is action.** End with "Tomorrow, before you…" — the book calls itself an action book.
- **Sincerity, always.** Carnegie insists over and over: this is not a bag of tricks, it's a new way of life. Any rule that could be read as manipulation gets that warning.

**Don't**
- Quote the book at length or copy its sentences. Retell stories in our own words; one short famous line (a few words) is the most we keep.
- Sound like a textbook ("Principle 4 states…") or a hype video.
- Invent stories, studies or numbers. If it isn't in the book, it isn't in the course.
- Use African or US-only framing for practice scenarios: Teyro is global, prices in US dollars.

**Sample — Lesson 1 opening, in the voice**

> New York, May 1931. A hundred and fifty police officers surround a top-floor apartment. Inside, a gunman crouches behind a chair, firing back. Ten thousand people watch from the street.
>
> And while the bullets fly, he writes a letter. Do you know what he says about himself? That under his coat beats a tired heart, but a kind one, a heart that would never hurt anybody.
>
> A kind heart. From a man who had shot a police officer for asking to see his licence.
>
> Now, here's the question. If a killer doesn't blame himself, what about the people you and I deal with every day?

**Microcopy in the voice**
- Correct: "That's it. That's exactly what Schwab would have done."
- Wrong: "Natural, isn't it? That's the reply most of us would give. Now look at what it does to the other person."
- Mission: "Try it once before tonight. Just once. Then come back and tell me what happened."

## 4. Course-wide facts to state once (Lesson 1 or course details)

- Carnegie's research note: in engineering, about 15% of financial success came from technical knowledge and about 85% from skill with people.
- Rockefeller said he'd pay more for the ability to deal with people than for any other ability.
- The book grew out of fifteen years of classes where adults tested the rules and reported back.
