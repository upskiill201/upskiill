## A different style: a late-night radio phone-in

Corporate Jargon followed one intern's week-by-week story. The Art of Small Talk followed one person through two months. This course does something different: **it's an anthology, framed as a radio show.**

**Tey FM** is a late-night phone-in show hosted by Tey: *"the station that listens back."* Every chapter, a different caller rings in with the same problem: **nobody listens to me.** Each caller is someone from the shared cast, in a different country and a different situation. The learner is Tey's **producer**: you screen the call, coach the caller between segments, and run the show.

- **Chapter = one caller's case.** The map banner shows "Caller 1: Clinton", "Caller 2: Arjun" and so on, instead of weeks.
- **Lesson 1 of each chapter = "On air":** the call comes in and we hear the problem in the caller's own words.
- **Lessons 2–9** are the coaching segments, each with a recurring radio name:
  - "Myth or Mic Drop" (myth or fact)
  - "Rewind" (before/after: we replay the caller's original message, then the fixed one)
  - "The Studio Whiteboard" (step-by-step)
  - "Producer's Notes" (cards)
  - "Line Check" (tap to explore)
- **Checkpoint = "The Phone-In Quiz".**
- **Unit Challenge = "The Call Back":** a week later the caller rings back live and tells us how it went. You coach them through the real moment as it happens.
- **Final Challenge = "The Live Special":** all five callers on air at once, plus Miracle's big conversation, live.

**Running jokes:**
- Tey's over-dramatic radio voice.
- Fake sponsor breaks: "This hour of Tey FM is brought to you by people who say 'per my last email'."
- The **Ignore-o-Meter**, which rates how ignored each caller is ("Clinton: muted by 40 people. A new record.").
- Callers from earlier chapters ringing back with updates.

**Why it works for this topic:** radio is all about being listened to. Nobody can see you, people can switch off at any second, and every second has to earn attention. The show *is* the lesson.

## What the research says (the course's backbone)

| Finding | Source | Used in |
|---|---|---|
| **Listening first earns you a hearing.** People who feel truly listened to become less defensive and more open-minded. | Itzchakov, Kluger & Castro (2017), *Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin*; Kluger et al. (2024) meta-analysis, *Journal of Business and Psychology* | Ch1 L3, Ch5 |
| **The curse of knowledge.** In a Stanford study, people tapped well-known songs; tappers predicted listeners would name 50% of them, and listeners named about 2.5%. Experts can't "un-hear" what they know. | Newton (1990), Stanford dissertation (widely reported, e.g. Heath & Heath) | Ch2 L2 |
| **Stories beat statistics for memory.** In a Stanford class exercise, 63% of listeners remembered the stories from short speeches; 5% remembered any single statistic. | Heath & Heath, *Made to Stick* (2007) — a classroom exercise, presented as such | Ch2 |
| **Working memory holds about four chunks.** Miller's famous "seven" was a rough figure; modern research suggests about four. Say less, in groups. | Cowan (2001), *Behavioral and Brain Sciences* | Ch2 L6 |
| **Your voice carries your intelligence.** Recruiters who *heard* a candidate's pitch rated them more competent, thoughtful and intelligent, and more hireable, than recruiters who read the same words. | Schroeder & Epley (2015), *Psychological Science* | Ch3 L2 |
| **Vocal confidence persuades.** Speakers who varied their volume and pitch came across as more confident, and more persuasive, without seeming less sincere. | Van Zant & Berger (2020), *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* | Ch3 |
| **The illusion of transparency.** Nervous speakers think their nerves are far more visible than they are. Simply knowing this helped speakers feel and perform better. | Savitsky & Gilovich (2003), *Journal of Experimental Social Psychology* | Ch3 |
| **Eye contact can backfire on sceptics.** With listeners who disagreed, more eye contact went with *less* persuasion. | Chen, Minson, Schöne & Heinrichs (2013), *Psychological Science* | Ch3, Ch5 |
| **Interruptions aren't equal.** In one study, both men and women interrupted female conversation partners more. | Hancock & Rubin (2015), *Journal of Language and Social Psychology* — one study, presented carefully | Ch4 |
| **Pushy language backfires (reactance).** "You must" and other controlling directives raise anger and counter-arguing, and reduce persuasion. | Brehm (1966); recent meta-analysis in *Human Communication Research* (2005–2024 studies) | Ch5 |
| **Myths busted:** the "8-second goldfish attention span" (traced to an unsupported statistic in a 2015 marketing report), and "only 7% of communication is words" (Mehrabian's findings were about feelings in conflicting messages; he has said he is misquoted). | BBC *More or Less* / University of Wisconsin myth-busting; Mehrabian's own clarification | Ch1 L2 |

**Handled carefully:**
- **Gender and interruptions:** one study, so it's presented as "research suggests" alongside practical tools that work for everyone. It's never framed as men against women.
- **Accents:** never something to fix. Clarity comes from pace and pauses (same rule as the other Communication courses).
- **Nerves:** practical and non-clinical, with signposting to help if anxiety is serious.
- **Family conversations (Ch5):** respectful of different family cultures. The goal is being *heard*, not winning, and the course never teaches manipulation.

## Chapters (lesson titles; Checkpoint at 5, Call Back at 10)

### Chapter 1 — Caller 1: Clinton · "Everyone muted me"
*Clinton runs a 40-person class group chat and talks the most in every room, and everyone has muted him. Tey shows him that being heard starts with listening, having one point, and saying less. **Call Back:** Clinton has 60 seconds to win votes in the first-year class rep election.*

1. On air: "Everyone muted me" (Story) · 2. Myth or Mic Drop: attention myths (goldfish, 7% words, louder = heard) (Myth or Fact) · 3. Listen first, get heard second (Chat with Tey) · 4. Find your one point (Step-by-step) · **5 Phone-In Quiz** · 6. Rewind: forty messages vs one (Before/After) · 7. Line Check: Clinton's 3-minute voice note (Tap to explore) · 8. Timing: when people can actually listen (Card deck) · 9. Talk less, say more (Reading) · **10 Call Back: the 60-second speech**

### Chapter 2 — Caller 2: Arjun · "They ignored my warning"
*Arjun warned a client about a bug, in paragraph four, in technical terms. They launched anyway, and it crashed. Tey shows him the curse of knowledge, putting the answer first, and making ideas stick. **Call Back:** two minutes on a call with the client's CEO to stop a second disaster.*

1. On air: "I told them in paragraph four" (Story) · 2. The curse of knowledge (Reading) · 3. Answer first, details after (Before/After) · 4. Say it so a 12-year-old gets it (Chat with Tey) · **5 Phone-In Quiz** · 6. Three things, not twelve (Card deck) · 7. Stories stick, statistics slip (Myth or Fact) · 8. Make numbers people can feel (Step-by-step) · 9. Line Check: the email that finally got read (Tap to explore) · **10 Call Back: two minutes with the CEO**

### Chapter 3 — Caller 3: David · "I'm the quiet one"
*David had the best idea in his group project. He said it quietly. Someone louder said it five minutes later and got the credit. Tey shows him what his voice can do, and that his nerves are far less visible than he thinks. **Call Back:** David presents the group project to the professor.*

1. On air: "They took my idea" (Story) · 2. Your voice carries your intelligence (Myth or Fact) · 3. Volume, pace and the power of the pause (Step-by-step) · 4. Nobody can see your nerves (Reading) · **5 Phone-In Quiz** · 6. Speak early, speak once, speak clearly (Chat with Tey) · 7. Eye contact: more isn't always better (Card deck) · 8. Rewind: the mumble vs the headline (Before/After) · 9. Line Check: David's notes card (Tap to explore) · **10 Call Back: the presentation**

### Chapter 4 — Caller 4: Lucía · "My team talks over me"
*Lucía is newly promoted. Diego, a senior colleague, interrupts her in every meeting, and her updates disappear into the noise. Tey shows her how to hold the floor, handle interruptions and run a room people listen in. **Call Back:** Lucía's first team planning meeting, with Diego in it.*

1. On air: "Diego finishes my sentences" (Story) · 2. What research says about interruptions (Reading) · 3. Holding the floor, politely (Card deck) · 4. Rewind: the update nobody heard (Before/After) · **5 Phone-In Quiz** · 6. When someone takes your idea (Chat with Tey) · 7. Run the meeting so everyone gets heard (Step-by-step) · 8. Firm isn't rude (Myth or Fact) · 9. Line Check: Lucía's follow-up email (Tap to explore) · **10 Call Back: the planning meeting**

### Chapter 5 — Caller 5: Miracle · "Will my family hear me?"
*Miracle wants to turn down a safe bank job for a startup in Lagos. Her parents want the bank. Tey shows her how to be heard by people who love her and disagree with her: listen first, don't push, find what you share. **Final Challenge, the Live Special:** all five callers on air, and Miracle's family dinner, live.*

1. On air: "They won't hear me out" (Story) · 2. Why pushing makes people push back (Myth or Fact) · 3. Listen to them first, really (Chat with Tey) · 4. Find what you both want (Step-by-step) · **5 Phone-In Quiz** · 6. Ask, don't tell (Before/After) · 7. Pick your moment (Card deck) · 8. When they still say no (Reading) · 9. Line Check: Miracle's opening line (Tap to explore) · **10 Final Challenge: the Live Special**

Every chapter uses all 8 Learn formats, and no two lessons in a row share a main format.
