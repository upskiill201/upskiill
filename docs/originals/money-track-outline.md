## Why this story will be hard to put down

The founder's brief: money courses that people can't drop. Here's how the story does that:

1. **One person, one year, three courses.** Each course is a full story with its own ending, so anyone can start with any of them. Played in order, they form one longer story: Kofi's first year with his own money.
2. **Real stakes, not lectures.** Every chapter has a problem that costs Kofi something he cares about: his pride with his mum, a laptop he needs for his degree, a friend in trouble, his football boots.
3. **A cliffhanger at the end of every chapter.** Lesson 10 (the Unit Challenge) gets Kofi through the chapter's problem, and its last screen opens the next one: a phone call from Mum, a laptop that won't switch on, a debt collector messaging a friend. The map banner for the next chapter is the hook.
4. **The map counts the money.** Chapter banners are numbers, so learners watch them move: "Day 12: GH₵63 left" → "Day 30". "GH₵0 of GH₵3,500" → "Paid in full". "2:14 a.m." → "Black Friday".
5. **One running device per course.** Lesson 9 of every chapter shows Kofi's sheet, jar or list growing from v1 to v5. Learners tap through it, as with Clinton's evidence board.
6. **The learner decides.** Every chapter has a Story lesson with a real choice: tell Mum or not, take the instant loan, go on the trip, lend Yaw money, buy the boots. Wrong choices play out (gently) and then get fixed.
7. **The learner's own money, every time.** Every Reflect turns the lesson onto the learner's own life: "Open your mobile money history. What did you spend on yesterday?" By the end they've built their own budget, savings plan and pause list.

## What the research says (all three courses)

| Finding | Source | Used in |
|---|---|---|
| Tracking progress toward a goal makes reaching it more likely, and more so when it's written down | Harkin et al. (2016), *Psychological Bulletin*, meta-analysis of 138 studies | C1 Ch1, Ch5 |
| Money runs short faster than you planned, because people underestimate future costs and time | Planning fallacy: Buehler, Griffin & Ross (1994) | C1 Ch3 |
| Being short of money takes up mental "bandwidth" and narrows focus to today ("tunnelling") | Mullainathan & Shafir, *Scarcity* (2013); Mani et al. (2013), *Science* | C1 Ch2, C2 Ch1 |
| People sort money into mental "pots" and treat each pot differently | Mental accounting: Thaler (1985, 1999) | C1 Ch3, Ch5 |
| Paying by card or phone hurts less than paying cash, so people spend more | Prelec & Loewenstein (1998), "The red and the black"; Prelec & Simester (2001) | C1 Ch4, C3 Ch3 |
| 50/30/20 as a starting rule of thumb (and why students usually need to bend it) | Warren & Warren Tyagi, *All Your Worth* (2005) | C1 Ch3 |
| New starts (a new month, a new semester) make change more likely | Fresh-start effect: Dai, Milkman & Riis (2014) | C1 Ch5, C2 Ch5 |
| "If X, then I'll do Y" plans make habits stick | Implementation intentions: Gollwitzer (1999) | C1 Ch5, C3 Ch5 |
| Saving first, automatically, beats saving what's left | Save More Tomorrow: Thaler & Benartzi (2004) | C2 Ch3 |
| A savings account you can't easily touch raised savings for people who chose one | Ashraf, Karlan & Yin (2006), commitment savings, the Philippines | C2 Ch3 |
| Low-income families saved more when money was split and labelled for a goal | Soman & Cheema (2011), *Journal of Marketing Research*, earmarking study in India | C2 Ch3 |
| Simple reminders increased savings | Karlan, McConnell, Mullainathan & Zinman (2016) | C2 Ch3 |
| We prefer "now" far more than we will later (present bias) | Laibson (1997); O'Donoghue & Rabin (1999) | C2 Ch1, C3 Ch1 |
| Seeing your future self makes you save more | Hershfield et al. (2011) | C2 Ch5 |
| Susu and rotating savings groups: how they work and why they're used worldwide | Besley, Coate & Loury (1993); traditional susu collector practice in Ghana | C2 Ch3 |
| Impulse buying: a sudden strong urge to buy now | Rook (1987), *Journal of Consumer Research* | C3 Ch1 |
| The brain's "want" signal fires when you see a product; seeing the price triggers a "pain" signal | Knutson et al. (2007), *Neuron* | C3 Ch1 |
| Shopping does lift mood in the short term, which is why it works as a habit | Atalay & Meloy (2011); Rick, Pereira & Burson (2014) | C3 Ch2 |
| Scarcity, social proof and the other persuasion levers | Cialdini, *Influence* | C3 Ch3 |
| Anchors ("was GH₵900") change what feels cheap | Tversky & Kahneman (1974) | C3 Ch3 |
| The decoy effect | Huber, Payne & Puto (1982) | C3 Ch3 |
| Dark patterns were found on about 1 in 10 shopping websites studied | Mathur et al. (2019), 11,000 shopping sites | C3 Ch3 |
| Experiences tend to make people happier than things | Van Boven & Gilovich (2003) | C3 Ch5 |

Every figure is checked again, and dated in the lesson notes, as it's written. No lesson names a bank, app, loan company or product (playbook §9).

## How "practical" works

No code. Practice is built on Kofi's numbers and then turned on the learner's own:
- sort transactions into fixed, flexible, need and want;
- work out a weekly budget, a sinking fund or the total cost of "small payments" (round numbers, phone-calculator friendly), and type the answer;
- spot the leak, the trick or the trigger in a real-looking screen;
- chat scenarios: saying no to Yaw, talking to Mum, answering a seller;
- every chapter's Reflect asks about the learner's own money, and each course's final challenge is a real project: your budget, your savings plan, your pause rules.

Format rule, as in the Business set: each chapter uses all eight Learn formats once across lessons 1–4 and 6–9, with lesson 9 always the device.

## Safety (playbook §9)

- General principles only. No product, bank, app or loan company is named or recommended, and there is no investment advice.
- Loan and "pay later" lessons explain how costs work, using made-up but realistic numbers, and point to free help where it exists, without naming providers.
- Tey never shames spending. The tone is "everyone does this, here's why, here's a tool".
- No gambling content (it's a different, more serious topic).

## What the founder is approving

1. The three titles, one storyline across all three (Kofi's money year at KNUST), the cast (Kofi leads; Efua, Yaw, Miracle by voice note, Mum narrated) and the cedi numbers.
2. 5 chapters × 10 lessons per course, a new **Personal Finance** category, no code.
3. The chapter and lesson plans above, including the cliffhanger at the end of every chapter.
4. Build order: add the category, write course 1 chapter by chapter, committing each one, then courses 2 and 3, then the covers. Nothing is pushed until you say so.
