## The storyline: five weeks to the review

Ada (24, Lagos) is a junior product designer at a fintech startup. She says yes to everything. In one week she:
- agrees to a second project with no change in pay;
- accepts her landlord's rent rise from ₦1,200,000 to ₦1,500,000 a year by text in four minutes;
- tells a bakery owner she'll design her logo "for whatever you think is fair".

She earns **₦350,000 a month**. Her six-month review with her manager, Tobi, is in five weeks. Lucía, the marketing lead at a client company, sees Ada's work and tells her she's underpaid. The course follows Ada through five weeks and four negotiations:
1. the bakery quote,
2. the rent,
3. a household win-win (the flat's one parking space and the generator fuel),
4. the salary review.

**What keeps it easy to follow:**
- **Ada's ask card.** Lesson 9 of every chapter shows her one-page negotiation plan, v1 to v5: what she wants, why, her walk-away, her alternative, their side, her opening number and her trades.
- **The same people.** Lucía mentors by video call. Remi is her friend on WhatsApp. Tey teaches. Tobi, Mr Adeyemi (landlord), Mrs Okon (bakery) and Ngozi (flatmate) are narrated.
- **The learner's job:** spot Ada's mistakes, rewrite her messages, and plan a real ask of their own. Every Reflect is about the learner's own life.

**Money:** naira, round numbers. All salary and rent figures are fictional but plausible, and each lesson's `notes` says so.

## What the research says

| Finding | Source | Used in |
|---|---|---|
| Few people negotiate a first offer; those who do earn more, and the gap compounds. | Babcock & Laschever, *Women Don't Ask* (2003). Carnegie Mellon graduates: about 7% of women vs 57% of men negotiated; negotiating raised starting pay by about 7.4% | Ch1 L3, L8 |
| People underestimate how often others will say yes to a direct request. | Flynn & Lake (2008), *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* | Ch1 L7 |
| Asking for advice makes you look more competent, not less (on hard tasks, from relevant people). | Brooks, Gino & Schweitzer (2015), *Management Science* | Ch1 L7 |
| Saying "I'm excited" beats "I'm calm" before a stressful task. | Brooks (2014), *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General* | Ch1 L4 |
| Interests vs positions; BATNA; objective criteria; separate the people from the problem. | Fisher, Ury & Patton, *Getting to Yes* (1981/2011) | Ch2, Ch4 |
| First offers act as anchors and predict the final deal. | Galinsky & Mussweiler (2001), *JPSP* | Ch3 L2 |
| An ambitious "bolstering" range can do as well as a single number, and seems more polite; a range that starts with a concession does worse. | Ames & Mason (2015), *JPSP* | Ch3 L6 |
| A reason ("because...") makes small requests more likely to be accepted. | Langer, Blank & Chanowitz (1978), the photocopier study | Ch3 L7 |
| Trading issues you value differently creates value (logrolling); the orange story. | Pruitt (1981); Mary Parker Follett; *Getting to Yes* | Ch4 |
| Making several offers at once (MESOs) gets information and better deals. | Leonardelli et al. (2019), *Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes*; Medvec & Galinsky (2005) | Ch4 L6 |
| Women can face backlash for asking. A short, relational reason ("I want to keep doing work the team relies on") reduces it. | Bowles, Babcock & Lai (2007); Bowles & Babcock (2013) | Ch5 L6 |
