## The storyline: calm with patients, frozen with colleagues

Grace (44, Manchester) spent 18 years as a ward nurse. She can calm a frightened patient at 3 a.m. Now a UX researcher at Kinwell, a health-tech company, she freezes when a colleague is angry with her. Then a launch deadline pits her research against Femi's code:
- Femi snaps at her in stand-up.
- The new product manager, Callum, criticises her report in front of the team: "This is just opinions."
- A misread Slack message turns into a two-day cold war.
- Priya, her manager, keeps adding work.
- At home, Jayden (17) borrows the car without asking.

**What keeps it easy to follow:**
- **Grace's ward rules.** Lesson 9 of every chapter shows her one-page "ward rules for the office", v1 to v5. These are nursing habits she translates for work ("check the chart before you act" becomes "check what they meant before you reply").
- **The same people.** Priya (manager, a quiet introvert) and Femi (a talker who overdoes it) speak. Jayden (her son) is the home practice ground. Arjun joins calls. Callum is narrated. Tey teaches.

## What the research says

| Finding | Source | Used in |
|---|---|---|
| Five conflict modes: competing, collaborating, compromising, avoiding, accommodating. Each fits some situations. | Thomas & Kilmann (1974), Thomas-Kilmann Conflict Mode Instrument | Ch1 |
| Relationship conflict hurts teams; task conflict is mixed. It can help decisions when it stays about the work. | De Dreu & Weingart (2003); De Wit, Greer & Jehn (2012), meta-analyses | Ch1 |
| A harsh start predicts a bad ending; a soft start-up helps. | Gottman's research on conflict conversations | Ch2 |
| Putting feelings into words reduces the brain's alarm response. | Lieberman et al. (2007) | Ch2 |
| "Go to the balcony" before reacting. | Ury, *Getting Past No* | Ch2 |
| People overestimate how well their tone comes across in writing. Senders expected about 78% of readers to spot sarcasm; about 56% did. | Kruger, Epley, Parker & Ng (2005), *JPSP* | Ch3 |
| The ladder of inference: we jump from data to stories to conclusions. | Argyris; Senge, *The Fifth Discipline* | Ch3 |
| Intent vs impact; the three conversations (what happened, feelings, identity). | Stone, Patton & Heen, *Difficult Conversations* (1999) | Ch3, Ch4 |
| Three feedback triggers (truth, relationship, identity); separate appreciation, coaching and evaluation. | Stone & Heen, *Thanks for the Feedback* (2014) | Ch4 |
| Observation, feeling, need, request. | Rosenberg, *Nonviolent Communication* | Ch5 |
